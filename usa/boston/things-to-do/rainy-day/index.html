It rains in Boston about one day in three, in every month of the year, and the rain here has a particular quality: sideways off the harbor, with a wind that turns umbrellas inside out on Boylston Street. The good news is that Boston is a city of the indoors as much as the outdoors. It has two of the best art museums in the country across the street from each other, a public library reading room people travel to see, a 19th-century market hall full of food, three independent cinemas within a Red Line ride, and a set of enclosed walkways in Back Bay that let you cross half a neighborhood without getting wet.

This guide is for the day the forecast turns. It covers the museums worth a full day, the libraries and bookstores, the indoor markets, the cinemas, the covered walkways, bowling and games and escape rooms, the cafés and pubs where you can wait out a downpour, and a rain-proof one-day itinerary that moves from museum to lunch to library to dinner with as little sidewalk as possible.

Every place has an address and the nearest T stop, and we've noted where the walk from the station is more than a couple of minutes. Hours change; if it matters, check before you go.

01

Museums: the best indoor things to do in Boston

Boston's museums are where a rainy day goes first. Two of them, the MFA and the Gardner, are neighbors in the Fenway and fill a full day between them. Here is the set, with the free hours.

Museum of Fine Arts

465 Huntington Ave, FenwayMuseum of Fine Arts (Green E), across the streetWed–Fri 10–10 (some galleries), Sat–Tue 10–5; check before you goAdults $27; Wed after 4pm pay what you wish

The MFA is one of the largest art museums in the country, with about half a million objects, and it is the single best rainy-day destination in Boston because you cannot finish it. Pick a wing: the Art of the Americas for Copley, Sargent, Homer and the Colonial silver; the Japanese galleries and the Buddhist temple room; the Egyptian collection; or the Impressionists, which include more Monets than any museum outside Paris. Two cafés and a restaurant mean you never have to leave. Wednesday evenings are voluntary admission and quieter than weekends.

Local tip: the Green Line E stop is directly in front of the Huntington Avenue entrance, so the walk in the rain is about 40 feet.

Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum

25 Evans Way, FenwayMuseum of Fine Arts (Green E), 5-minute walkWed–Mon 11–5, Thu until 9; closed TueAdults about $22

The Gardner is a Venetian palazzo built in 1903 around a courtyard garden under a glass roof, and rain on that glass is the best sound in Boston. Isabella Stewart Gardner arranged every painting, tapestry and chair herself and stipulated in her will that nothing could be moved, which is why the empty frames from the 1990 theft still hang where the Vermeer and the Rembrandts were. The Renzo Piano wing has a café and a concert hall. It's a five-minute walk from the MFA, and the two together are the full-day version.

ICA Boston

25 Harbor Shore Dr, SeaportCourthouse (Silver Line SL1/SL2), 5-minute walkTue–Sun 10–5, Thu–Fri until 9Adults about $20; free Thu 5–9pm

The Institute of Contemporary Art is a glass building cantilevered over the harbor, and on a grey day the fourth-floor gallery's floor-to-ceiling window onto the water is its own exhibit. The collection is contemporary and the shows rotate often. Thursday nights are free and busy; a rainy Thursday is one of the better cheap evenings in the city. The Seaport's restaurants and Time Out-style food halls are a few minutes away, mostly under awnings.

Museum of Science

1 Science Park, on the Charles River DamScience Park/West End (Green), covered walkway into the buildingDaily 9–5, Fri until 9Adults about $29; planetarium and Omni extra

The Museum of Science straddles the Charles River between Boston and Cambridge and has the Green Line station attached to it, which on a rainy day counts for a lot. It is enormous, with the Theater of Electricity's indoor lightning show, a planetarium, a butterfly garden, an Omni dome theater and several hundred hands-on exhibits. It's the first choice for a wet day with kids and a solid one without them. Budget four hours.

Harvard's museums

Harvard Art Museums, 32 Quincy St; Natural History, 26 Oxford St, CambridgeHarvard (Red), 5–10 minute walkArt Museums Tue–Sun 10–5, free; Natural History daily 9–5, about $15

The Harvard Art Museums have been free to everyone since 2023: three collections under a Renzo Piano glass roof, five minutes from the Red Line. The Harvard Museum of Natural History, on the other side of the Yard, has the Glass Flowers, a 4,000-piece collection of botanical models made in Germany between 1887 and 1936 that is one of the strangest and most beautiful things in Boston. Between the two you can spend a full rainy afternoon, and Harvard Square's bookstores and cafés are a block away.

New England Aquarium

1 Central WharfAquarium (Blue), 2-minute walkDaily 9–5 or 6, check before you goAdults about $39

The Aquarium's four-story Giant Ocean Tank, with a spiral ramp winding around it, is the center of the building and worth the price on its own. Penguins on the ground floor, a touch tank, the IMAX next door. It's expensive and crowded on rainy weekends, so go on a weekday if you can. The harbor seals in the outdoor tank by the entrance are free to watch from the plaza, rain or shine.

02

Libraries and bookstores

Boston Public Library and Bates Hall

700 Boylston St, Copley SquareCopley (Green), 1-minute walkMon–Thu 9–8, Fri–Sat 9–5, Sun 11–5Free

The McKim Building of the Boston Public Library opened in 1895 and is the most beautiful public room in the city. Bates Hall, the main reading room, has a 50-foot barrel-vaulted ceiling, green-shaded lamps and long oak tables, and anyone can sit down with a book or a laptop. The Sargent murals on the third floor, the Abbey Room with its Holy Grail frieze, and the central courtyard with its arcade are all free. The Newsfeed Café by the Boylston Street entrance has coffee and a bar. On a rainy afternoon it is the best place in Boston to be.

Harvard Square bookstores

Harvard Book Store, 1256 Massachusetts Ave; Grolier, 6 Plympton St; the Coop, 1400 Massachusetts AveHarvard (Red), all within 3 minutesHarvard Book Store open until 10pmFree to browse

Harvard Square has the densest set of bookstores in New England, all close enough to the T to dash between. Harvard Book Store is the big independent one, with a used-book basement. The Grolier Poetry Book Shop, open since 1927, is the size of a living room and the best poetry shop in the country. Raven Used Books on JFK Street is for academic remainders. Guide: Harvard Square.

Brattle Book Shop

9 West St, Downtown CrossingPark Street (Red/Green) or Downtown Crossing (Orange/Red)Mon–Sat 9–5:30; check before you goFree to browse

The Brattle Book Shop has been selling used and rare books since 1825 and is one of the oldest bookstores in the country. Three floors inside; on dry days the famous outdoor sale lot next door has $1 to $5 carts under the mural. On rainy days the carts are covered and you go inside, which is the better half anyway.

The Boston Athenaeum and Trident Booksellers

Athenaeum, 10½ Beacon St; Trident, 338 Newbury StPark Street (Red/Green) / Hynes (Green)Athenaeum first floor Tue–Sat, free; Trident daily 8am–midnight

The Boston Athenaeum, a members' library since 1807, opens its first floor to the public with rotating exhibitions and a reading room facing the Granary Burying Ground. Trident on Newbury Street is a bookstore with a full café and breakfast all day, open until midnight, and it is where Back Bay goes when it rains.

03

Indoor markets and food halls

Boston Public Market and Quincy Market

Boston Public Market, 100 Hanover St; Quincy Market, 4 S Market StHaymarket (Orange/Green), directly beneath the Public MarketPublic Market daily 8–6 (Sun from 10); Quincy Market daily 10–9Lunch $10–20

The Boston Public Market is an indoor market of about 30 Massachusetts producers, with the Haymarket T station underneath it, so you can arrive without seeing the sky. Cider donuts, oysters, cheese, coffee, a bakery, a dumpling counter. Two blocks away, Quincy Market is the 1826 granite hall behind Faneuil Hall, with a long colonnade of food stalls and a central rotunda for eating. It's touristy, but it is dry, warm and open until 9pm, and the ground floor of Faneuil Hall next door has free National Park Service talks.

Time Out Market

401 Park Dr, FenwayFenway (Green D), 3-minute walkDaily from about 11am until 10pm or later$15–25 per person

Time Out Market occupies the ground floor of the old Sears building in the Fenway, a block from the MFA, with a dozen counters from established Boston kitchens, two bars and communal tables. It's the natural lunch on the museum day and the place to go when a group can't agree. The building also has a Target and a small cinema upstairs, which extends the dry time considerably.

High Street Place and Hub Hall

High Street Place, 100 High St; Hub Hall, 80 Causeway StSouth Station (Red) / North Station (Orange/Green)Weekday lunch and dinner; Hub Hall late on event nights

High Street Place is a food hall in the atrium between two Financial District towers, with about 20 vendors and a big central bar; it's a weekday lunch place mostly. Hub Hall, at North Station under the TD Garden, is the newer one and connects directly to the Orange and Green Line platforms and the commuter rail, which makes it the driest dinner in the city.

04

Cinemas for a rainy afternoon

Coolidge Corner Theatre

290 Harvard St, BrooklineCoolidge Corner (Green C), across the streetDaily; check the programAbout $14–16

The Coolidge opened in 1933 as an Art Deco movie palace and is now a nonprofit that shows first-run independent films, 35mm repertory, midnight cult movies and a long-running science-on-screen series. A 2024 expansion added two screens and a rooftop lounge. It is a two-stop ride from Kenmore on the C branch and the station is across the street. Coolidge Corner itself has a bookstore and a dozen restaurants for after.

The Brattle Theatre

40 Brattle St, Harvard Square, CambridgeHarvard (Red), 2-minute walkDaily; often double featuresAbout $14

The Brattle is a single-screen repertory cinema in a converted 1890 hall that has been showing films since 1953 and is credited with inventing the Casablanca revival. Double features, retrospectives, a rear-projection screen and a balcony. It's a Harvard Square institution and the most Boston way to spend a rainy afternoon short of the library.

Landmark Kendall Square Cinema

355 Binney St, CambridgeKendall/MIT (Red), 10-minute walkDailyAbout $15

Kendall Square Cinema is a nine-screen theater specializing in first-run independent and foreign films, and it is where new documentaries and festival films open in Boston. The walk from the Red Line is the longest of the three, so this is the one to take a bus or a rideshare to in a real downpour. Cambridge Brewing Company is next door.

AMC Boston Common

175 Tremont StBoylston (Green), 1-minute walkDaily, late showsAbout $17

The big downtown multiplex, 19 screens, IMAX, across from Boston Common and next to the Theater District. It's the practical choice on a rainy night for a first-run blockbuster, and Chinatown's restaurants are two blocks away for a late dinner.

05

The Prudential and Copley Place covered walkways

Prudential Center to Copley Place, without going outside

800 Boylston St (Prudential) to 100 Huntington Ave (Copley Place)Prudential (Green E) and Back Bay (Orange) both connect indoorsShops roughly 10–8, walkways longerFree

Back Bay has the closest thing Boston has to an indoor city. The Prudential (Green E) station opens into the Prudential Center's shopping arcade. From there, an enclosed glass bridge over Huntington Avenue takes you to Copley Place, which connects to the Westin and Marriott hotels and to Back Bay station on the Orange Line. Another bridge from the Prudential reaches the Hynes Convention Center and the Sheraton. You can walk from Huntington Avenue to Dartmouth Street, get a coffee, browse, eat at a food court or a restaurant, and go up to View Boston on the 50th floor, all under a roof.

Local tip: View Boston is a good rainy-day gamble: the clouds often clear over the harbor first, and low cloud rolling over the Back Bay from the 52nd floor is its own show. Check the visibility before you buy.

Downtown Crossing and Faneuil Hall on foot

Downtown Crossing (Orange/Red) to Haymarket (Orange/Green)Downtown Crossing / Government Center / HaymarketMostly street level, with arcadesFree

Downtown doesn't have Back Bay's bridges, but it has short blocks and a lot of awnings. From Downtown Crossing, Macy's and the Corner Mall are indoors; from there it's three minutes to the Old State House and Faneuil Hall, whose ground floor and Quincy Market are inside. The Boston Public Market sits on top of the Haymarket station. Plan the day as a series of dry pockets and it works.

06

Bowling, games and escape rooms

Lucky Strike Fenway

145 Ipswich St, FenwayKenmore (Green) or Fenway (Green D), 5-minute walkDaily, afternoon to late; check before you goBowling about $8–10 per person per game plus shoes

Lucky Strike, behind the Green Monster, has bowling lanes, pool and billiards, arcade games, a full bar and a restaurant across two floors. It's the standard Boston rainy-night group plan and it's also fine for two. Weekend evenings get busy and loud; a weekday afternoon is a different, quieter place. Time Out Market and the MFA are both close.

Level99

Natick Mall, 1235 Worcester St, NatickCommuter rail to Natick Center, then a 15-min bus or rideshare; easier by carDaily; check hoursAround $30–45 per person for a session

Level99 is a warehouse-sized set of about 50 small physical and mental challenge rooms, two to eight minutes each, played in teams: climbing, puzzles, balance, speed. It is out at the Natick Mall, about 40 minutes west of the city, and it's the one entry here that is genuinely easier with a car. If you have a group and a whole rained-out day, it's worth the trip. Night Shift Brewing has a taproom inside.

Escape rooms

Boxaroo, 7 Water St, Downtown; Escape the Room, 33 West St; Trapology, 177 Tremont StState (Orange/Blue) / Park Street / Boylston (Green)Bookings by the hour, dailyAbout $30–40 per person

Downtown Boston has several escape rooms within a few blocks of Park Street. Boxaroo on Water Street is the most inventive, with original rooms built in-house. Trapology on Tremont Street has been running since 2015 and has a good record with mixed groups. Book ahead on rainy weekends, when everyone else has the same idea.

Kings Dining and Entertainment, and Flight Club

Kings, 60 Seaport Blvd; Flight Club, 60 Seaport BlvdCourthouse (Silver), 3-minute walkAfternoon to late; check before you go

Kings in the Seaport has bowling, a big bar and late food; Flight Club next door is social darts with automated scoring in a theatrical room. Neither is quiet. Both are a short covered walk from the Silver Line and the ICA, and the Seaport has plenty of restaurants under awnings for afterward.

07

Cozy cafés and pubs to wait out the rain

Sometimes the right plan is a corner table and a window. These have the windows.

Caffè Vittoria

290 Hanover St, North EndHaymarket (Orange/Green), 5-minute walkDaily from morning until lateEspresso $3–5

Open since 1929, Caffè Vittoria is the North End café with the espresso machines in the window, the tin ceiling and the old men arguing at the back. It has three rooms, a downstairs bar and no hurry. A cannoli from Modern Pastry two doors down is permitted. See the best cannoli in Boston.

Tatte and the Charles Street cafés

Tatte, 70 Charles St; Beacon Hill Books & Café, 71 Charles StCharles/MGH (Red), 3-minute walkRoughly 7am–7pm

Charles Street is narrow, brick and lined with awnings, which makes it one of the better rainy streets. Tatte's Beacon Hill café has the pastries and the crowds. Beacon Hill Books & Café across the street has five floors of bookshop and a proper café in the basement. Our list of the best cafés in Boston has the rest, and cafés for working covers the ones with outlets.

The Sevens Ale House

77 Charles St, Beacon HillCharles/MGH (Red)Daily, afternoon to 1amPints $7–9

The Sevens is a narrow Beacon Hill pub with dark wood, a dartboard and a menu of sandwiches, and it has been the neighborhood's rainy-afternoon bar since 1933. No TV noise to speak of, no rush. The Boston Common is two minutes away when the rain stops.

The Druid

1357 Cambridge St, Inman Square, CambridgeCentral (Red), 12-minute walk, or bus 91/69Daily, lunch until 1am

The Druid is a small Irish pub in Inman Square with a real fireplace, a good Guinness, a proper fish and chips and live traditional music some nights. It's the pub to be stuck in. Ranked in the best Irish pubs in Boston.

Oak Long Bar at the Fairmont Copley Plaza

138 St James Ave, Back BayCopley (Green), 2-minute walkDaily until midnight or laterCocktails $18–22

The grand hotel bar of Back Bay: a long oak room with brass, high ceilings and windows onto Copley Square and the library. Expensive, and worth one drink while it pours. The library is across the square and the Copley Place walkways start a block away.

09

Staying dry on the T

The T is the rainy-day tool. Several stations open directly into buildings: Haymarket into the Boston Public Market, Prudential into the Prudential Center, Back Bay into Copley Place via the Westin, North Station into Hub Hall and the TD Garden, Science Park into the Museum of Science, and South Station into its own concourse of food and shops. Downtown Crossing connects underground to the Park Street Red and Green Line platforms, so you can cross from the Orange Line to the Common's edge without surfacing.

The Green Line is the rainy-day line: it stops at the door of the MFA, the library (Copley), the Prudential, the Coolidge Corner Theatre, Symphony Hall and the Museum of Science. A single ride is $2.40 with a contactless card or a CharlieCard, and a 7-day pass is $22.50. Weekend track work is common, so check the alerts before you leave.

Boston rain rarely lasts all day. A typical wet day here has a dry window in the afternoon, so keep one outdoor thing in reserve for when it comes: the Harborwalk from Long Wharf, the Public Garden, or the Freedom Trail's northern half. Our guide to getting around Boston covers the rest, and things to do in Boston this weekend always has a rain fallback for the current week. FAQ

Rainy days in Boston: common questions

What is the best thing to do in Boston on a rainy day?

The Museum of Fine Arts and the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum, which are across the street from each other in the Fenway and together fill a full day. The Gardner's glass-roofed courtyard is at its best in rain. If you'd rather not pay, the Boston Public Library's Bates Hall is free and one of the most beautiful rooms in the country.

Can you walk around Boston indoors?

Partly. In Back Bay, the Prudential Center, Copley Place and the Hynes Convention Center are connected by enclosed walkways, so you can go from Huntington Avenue to Copley Square without going outside. Downtown, Quincy Market, the Boston Public Market and Faneuil Hall are within a block of each other and of the Haymarket T station.

Which Boston museums are free on rainy days?

The Harvard Art Museums are free every day. The ICA is free on Thursday evenings from 5 to 9pm, the MFA is pay-what-you-wish on Wednesdays after 4pm, and the MIT Museum is free on the last Sunday of the month. The Boston Public Library and the ground floor of Faneuil Hall are free all the time. See free things to do in Boston.

What can you do in Boston when it rains with kids?

The Museum of Science, the New England Aquarium, the Boston Children's Museum and the Harvard Museum of Natural History are the four big ones. Level99 in Natick and Lucky Strike in Fenway work for older kids and teenagers, and the Boston Public Library has free children's story times most days. More in our family guide.

Where can I see a movie in Boston when it rains?

The Coolidge Corner Theatre in Brookline is a 1933 Art Deco cinema showing independent and classic films. The Brattle in Harvard Square is a single-screen repertory house with double features. The Landmark Kendall Square Cinema in Cambridge shows first-run independent films on nine screens. AMC Boston Common is the big downtown multiplex.

How much does it rain in Boston?

Boston gets about 43 inches of precipitation a year, spread fairly evenly across the months, with around 10 to 12 wet days per month. Rain rarely lasts all day; a typical Boston rainy day has a dry window in the afternoon, which is worth planning around.
