Boston on a budget.
A real plan, with real prices.

What Boston actually costs per day, where to sleep cheaply, how to spend $22.50 on a week of transit, and a priced three-day itinerary you can follow as written.

By no bad days club, BostonUpdated September 8, 202614 min read

Boston has a reputation as an expensive city, and for one thing it is: the bed. Hotel rates here are among the highest in the country, and a mediocre downtown room in October can cost more than a good one in most European capitals. Everything after the bed is a different story. The city's biggest attraction is a free walk. Its most beautiful building is a public library. Four of its major museums have a free day or evening. The neighborhoods that matter are all within 40 minutes of each other on foot, and a week of unlimited transit costs $22.50.

So doing Boston on a budget is really two problems: solve the bed, then don't waste money on things that are free. This guide is built around that. It starts with an honest daily budget at three price levels, then goes through where to stay cheaply, how transit passes and fare-free buses work, which attractions and museums cost nothing, where to eat for under $15, what changes by season, and a three-day itinerary with the cost of every day added up.

Prices are what we saw in September 2026. Hotel rates swing hard with the calendar, so treat the accommodation numbers as a range and check the dates you actually want.

The short answer

The 10 ways to do Boston cheap

  1. Sleep at HI Boston, Found Hotel, or across the river in Cambridge
  2. Come between November and March, when rates drop
  3. Buy the $22.50 7-day pass and walk everything central
  4. Do the Freedom Trail, the parks and the Harborwalk for $0
  5. Time museums to their free days
  6. Eat in the North End at lunch, not dinner
  7. Use Chinatown, food halls and Haymarket for the rest
  8. Ride the Charlestown ferry instead of a harbor cruise
  9. See the skyline from East Boston, not a paid deck
  10. Skip the airport taxi, the duck boat and the tourist chowder

Two things stand out. First, the gap between shoestring and mid-budget is almost entirely the bed and the dinner. Second, the shoestring column is not miserable: it includes a day of transit, a museum on its free night, a real lunch in the North End and the best walk in the city. Boston is a place where spending less mostly means seeing the same things and sleeping somewhere plainer.

Sales tax is 6.25% on most goods but not on groceries or most clothing under $175. Restaurant meals carry a 7% meals tax in Boston, and tipping 18–20% is expected at sit-down places and bars, so a $30 menu price is really about $38. Factor that in when you read a menu.

02

Where to stay in Boston cheaply

The single biggest decision in a Boston budget. These are the real options, in roughly ascending order of price.

HI Boston hostel

19 Stuart St, Theater DistrictBoylston (Green) or Chinatown (Orange), 3-minute walkDorm beds roughly $50–80; private rooms more; check current rates

HI Boston is the city's main hostel and one of the best-located beds in Boston at any price: a purpose-built building on the edge of the Theater District, a five-minute walk from Boston Common, Chinatown and the start of the Freedom Trail. Dorms are 4 to 10 beds with lockers and en-suite bathrooms, breakfast is included, there is a large kitchen and a lounge, and the staff run free walking tours and events most days. Private rooms exist but at that price you should compare hotels. Dorms sell out weeks ahead in summer and around graduations; book early.

Found Hotel Boston Common

79 Boylston St, Theater DistrictBoylston (Green), 1-minute walkRooms roughly $110–200 depending on season and bathroom; check before you book

Found is a budget hotel in a 19th-century building facing Boston Common that runs somewhere between a hostel and a hotel: small private rooms, some with shared bathrooms down the hall and some en-suite, a shared kitchen and lounge, and a location that would cost three times as much next door. Rooms are compact and the building is old, so it is not for people who want space, but for a solo traveler or a couple who plan to be out all day it is the cheapest private room in central Boston most nights of the year.

Cambridge, Somerville and Brookline

Along the Red Line (Cambridge, Somerville) or Green Line (Brookline)Central, Harvard, Porter, Davis; Coolidge Corner, Brookline VillageHotels and rooms often 20–40% below downtown

The best budget move in Boston is to not sleep in Boston. Cambridge and Somerville on the Red Line and Brookline on the Green Line are 10 to 20 minutes from Downtown, have their own restaurants and nightlife, and have cheaper hotels, more short-term rooms and lower parking costs. Central Square and Porter Square in Cambridge and Davis Square in Somerville are the sweet spots: lively, well connected and residential enough that prices are lower. Coolidge Corner in Brookline is quieter and 15 minutes to Copley on the C branch. Our where to stay in Boston guide goes area by area.

Come in the off-season

Cheapest: January to early MarchNext: November and early DecemberMost expensive: May, October, Marathon weekend

Boston hotel rates can double between a Tuesday in February and a Saturday in October. Winter is cold but the city is at its most affordable, the museums and library are the point anyway, and the Freedom Trail is empty. Late November and early December get the holiday lights on the Common and Commonwealth Avenue with rates still low. If you must come in summer, aim for a weekday stay rather than a weekend, and avoid the week of any major university's commencement, usually mid-to-late May. 03

How to save on transit in Boston

Walk first. Boston is the most compact major city in the US. Downtown to the North End is 10 minutes on foot, Downtown to the Seaport 15, Downtown to Copley Square 20, and Copley to Fenway 25. If you are staying centrally, you may only need transit to reach Cambridge, the museums on Huntington Avenue, Charlestown or East Boston.

Buy the 7-day pass if you are here three days or more. A single subway ride is $2.40, and a local bus is $1.70. The 7-day LinkPass costs $22.50 and covers unlimited subway, local bus, the Silver Line and the inner-harbor ferries, including the Charlestown ferry. Ten rides pays for it. It loads onto a CharlieCard at any station's fare machine, or you can tap a contactless credit card or phone, which caps at the same daily rates. A 1-day pass is $11, which only makes sense if you are riding five or more times in a day.

Use the fare-free buses. Since 2022, routes 23, 28 and 29 have had no fare at all. They run from Ruggles and Nubian Square through Roxbury, Dorchester and Mattapan, which is useful for reaching Franklin Park, the Franklin Park Zoo area and the neighborhoods along Blue Hill Avenue that most visitors never see. They are not tourist routes, but they are real, and they are free.

Ride the ferry instead of a cruise. The MBTA's F4 ferry from Long Wharf to the Charlestown Navy Yard costs the same as a subway fare, is covered by the 7-day pass, and gives you the harbor-side skyline both ways. It is the cheapest boat trip in the city and the smart way home from the end of the Freedom Trail.

Bluebikes for the river and the Southwest Corridor. Boston's bike share is $2.95 for a single ride under 30 minutes or about $10 for a day pass with unlimited two-hour rides; check current pricing. Stations are everywhere central. The best car-free routes are the Esplanade and Memorial Drive along the Charles, and the Southwest Corridor park from Back Bay to Jamaica Plain.

Get from the airport for $2.40. Logan is on the Blue Line. Take the free Massport shuttle from your terminal to Airport station, then the Blue Line into Downtown in 10 minutes. The Silver Line SL1 bus from the terminals to South Station is also free from the airport in the inbound direction. A taxi or rideshare is $30–45 with airport fees and often slower in traffic. 04

Free attractions worth building days around

The best of Boston costs nothing, which is what makes a budget trip here feel like a normal trip. The Freedom Trail is a free 2.5-mile walk through 16 historic sites, and 13 of them are free to enter, including the State House, the burying grounds, Faneuil Hall, the Old North Church, the USS Constitution and the Bunker Hill Monument. Only the Old State House, the Old South Meeting House and the Paul Revere House charge, and the trail is excellent without them.

Beyond the trail: Boston Common and the Public Garden, the Charles River Esplanade for sunset, the Harborwalk from the Seaport to the North End, Bates Hall at the Boston Public Library, Beacon Hill's streets, Harvard Yard and the free Harvard Art Museums, Castle Island in South Boston, and the skyline from Piers Park in East Boston, which is a better view than any paid observation deck and costs one Blue Line ride. Each of those is a half day, and none of them requires a ticket.

We keep the complete list, with addresses, T stops and hours, in free things to do in Boston. If you are working from a budget of zero for attractions, that page plus this one is enough to fill a week. The things worth paying for in Boston, if you pay for anything, are a single museum on a non-free day, the Boston Harbor Islands ferry in summer, and a Fenway Park bleacher seat, and none of them are essential.

Two other things worth knowing. Many museums offer student and senior discounts with ID, usually $5 to $10 off. Eating in Boston for under $15

Boston restaurants are pricey at dinner, but the city has a deep bench of cheap lunches. The rule: eat your big meal at midday, and use these neighborhoods.

The North End at lunch

Hanover and Salem StreetsHaymarket (Orange/Green)Weekday lunch$5–15

The same restaurants that charge $30 a plate at dinner have lunch specials, and the cheapest food in the neighborhood is not at a restaurant at all. Galleria Umberto at 289 Hanover Street sells Sicilian pizza squares, arancini and calzones for a few dollars each, cash only, from 11am until it runs out. A cannoli at Mike's or Modern is under $6. Sandwiches at the salumerias on Salem Street are under $12.

Chinatown

Around Beach St and Harrison AveChinatown (Orange)Lunch and late night$8–15

Boston's Chinatown is small, dense and the cheapest sit-down dinner in the central city. Dumpling houses, noodle shops, dim sum at lunch, Vietnamese pho, and bakeries selling pork buns for a couple of dollars. Several places stay open past midnight. It is a five-minute walk from Boston Common and next door to HI Boston.

Food halls and markets

Boston Public Market, 100 Hanover St; Time Out Market, 401 Park Dr; Quincy MarketHaymarket; Fenway (Green D); Government CenterDaily$8–18

The Boston Public Market on the Greenway has about 30 local vendors under one roof and is good for a $10 lunch. Quincy Market is more expensive but fast and central. Time Out Market in Fenway is a collection of counter versions of well-known Boston restaurants at lower prices than their main rooms. On Fridays and Saturdays, Haymarket, the outdoor produce market on Blackstone Street, sells fruit and vegetables for a fraction of supermarket prices; if you have a kitchen at your hostel, this is where to shop.

Student neighborhoods

Allston, Central Square, Davis Square, Mission HillHarvard Ave (Green B); Central, Davis (Red); Brigham Circle (Green E)Any time$8–15

Boston has more than 150,000 college students, and the neighborhoods they live in are priced for them. Allston has Korean, Vietnamese, Taiwanese and Brazilian food along Harvard and Brighton Avenues. Central Square in Cambridge is the same idea with more variety. Davis Square in Somerville and Mission Hill near the hospitals are cheaper still. These are also where the cheap bars are.

Our full list, with the specific places and what to order, is in the best cheap eats in Boston. For happy hours, note that Massachusetts bans drink discounts by law, so "happy hour" in Boston means cheap food specials, not cheap drinks; details in the best happy hours in Boston.

Money-saving mistakes to avoid in Boston

Taking a taxi from the airport. It is $30–45 with fees and often slower than the Blue Line, which is $2.40 and 10 minutes to Downtown. The SL1 bus to South Station is free from the airport. There is no reason to drive.

Renting a car. Boston parking is $40–60 a night at hotels, the streets are a colonial maze, and every neighborhood you want is on the T or on foot. A car costs money and time here. If you want a day trip, the commuter rail goes to Salem, Rockport and Providence for under $15 each way.

Paying full price at a museum on the wrong day. The MFA on a Wednesday evening is pay-what-you-wish; on a Tuesday it is nearly $30. The ICA on Thursday evening is free; on Saturday it is $20. Check the table above and move your museum day.

Eating dinner in the North End and lunch somewhere cheap. Reverse it. The North End at lunch is a bargain and at dinner is a splurge with a line. Chinatown, Allston and Central Square are where cheap dinners live.

Buying a harbor cruise or a paid observation deck when you have a T pass. The Charlestown ferry is included in the pass and is the harbor cruise. Piers Park in East Boston is the skyline view, for the price of a Blue Line ride. Our best views of Boston guide lists 13 free ones.

Booking a downtown hotel in October or May without checking Cambridge. Rates across the river are routinely 20–40% lower for a room 12 minutes from Park Street.

Buying single rides for a four-day trip. Ten rides at $2.40 is $24; the 7-day pass is $22.50 and includes the ferry. Get the pass on day one.

Expecting a happy hour. Massachusetts law prohibits discounted drinks, so bars cannot run one. Look for food specials instead, and drink in the student neighborhoods where the base prices are lower.

Doing the Freedom Trail with a paid tour when the rangers are free. The National Park Service leads free walks along the Downtown section from Faneuil Hall in season. The paid costumed tours are good, but the free ones are led by people who do this for a living.

Skipping the library. The Boston Public Library's McKim Building is free, has the most beautiful room in the city, runs free art and architecture tours, and has clean bathrooms and wifi in the middle of Back Bay. It is the best rainy-hour plan in Boston and it costs nothing.

FAQ

Boston on a budget: common questions

How much does a trip to Boston cost per day?

A careful budget traveler staying in a hostel dorm and eating cheaply can do Boston for about $90 to $120 a day including a bed. A mid-budget visitor in a modest hotel or a Cambridge or Brookline room spends $200 to $280 a day. Sightseeing itself is cheap; the bed is the expensive part.

Is Boston expensive to visit?

Hotels are expensive, among the priciest in the US, and restaurants are on the high side. Almost everything else is cheap or free: the Freedom Trail, the parks, the Harborwalk, the library, several museums on their free days, and a 7-day T pass for $22.50. If you solve the bed, Boston is a cheap city to visit.

What is the cheapest way to get around Boston?

Walking. The central neighborhoods are all within 30 to 40 minutes of each other on foot. For everything else, a 7-day MBTA pass at $22.50 covers the subway, local buses and the Charlestown ferry, and the 23, 28 and 29 buses are fare-free. Bluebikes is $2.95 a ride or about $10 for a day pass.

Where can I stay cheaply in Boston?

HI Boston, the hostel at 19 Stuart Street downtown, is the cheapest well-located bed in the city. Found Hotel Boston Common at 79 Boylston Street is a budget hotel with small rooms and shared-bath options. Otherwise look in Cambridge, Somerville or Brookline on the Red or Green Lines, and travel between November and March when rates drop.

When is the cheapest time to visit Boston?

January through early March, when hotel rates are at their lowest and the museums and library are the main attractions anyway. November and early December are the next cheapest. Avoid graduation season in May, the Marathon in April, and foliage weekends in October, when hotels charge the most.

Which Boston museums are free?

The Harvard Art Museums are free every day. The ICA is free on Thursday evenings. The Museum of Fine Arts is pay-what-you-wish on Wednesday evenings. The MIT Museum is free on the last Sunday of the month. The USS Constitution Museum is by donation. Schedules pause for special exhibitions, so check before you go.
