By no bad days club, BostonUpdated September 8, 202614 min read

The Freedom Trail is the one thing almost every visitor to Boston does, and the thing most of them do badly. They start at noon, skip the burying grounds, lose the red line somewhere near Haymarket, and give up before the North End, which is where it gets good. That is a shame, because the trail is genuinely the best single walk in the city: 2.5 miles, 16 sites, and a route that happens to pass through Boston's four most interesting neighborhoods in the right order.

This guide is the version we give to friends. It covers what the trail actually is, how long it really takes, every one of the 16 stops in order with its address and whether it costs anything to go inside, a table you can use as a Freedom Trail map, and three ways to do it depending on how much time you have. Then the practical stuff: free ranger walks versus paid tours, where to eat without leaving the trail, and how to get back from Charlestown without walking.

Prices and hours are correct as of September 2026, but the sites are run by half a dozen different organizations and change their schedules seasonally, so check before you go if a specific interior matters to you.

The short answer

The Freedom Trail in 10 facts

  1. It is a 2.5-mile red brick line connecting 16 historic sites
  2. It starts on Boston Common, next to Park Street station
  3. It ends at the Bunker Hill Monument in Charlestown
  4. Walking it takes 1 hour; doing it properly takes 3–5
  5. Walking is free, and 13 of 16 stops are free to enter
  6. The Old State House, Old South Meeting House and Paul Revere House charge admission
  7. The National Park Service runs free ranger-led walks in season

  9. Take the ferry back from Charlestown, not the walk
  10. Short on time? Do Boston Common to Faneuil Hall in one hour

01

What the Freedom Trail is

The Freedom Trail is a marked walking route through central Boston that links 16 sites connected to the American Revolution and the city's colonial history. It was proposed in 1951 by a Boston Herald journalist named William Schofield, who noticed that visitors kept getting lost between historic buildings and suggested painting a line on the ground to connect them. The city did, and it has been there in some form ever since: red paint in some places, inlaid red brick in most, with bronze medallions marking each official stop.

The route runs from Boston Common in the center of the city, through Downtown and the Financial District, past Faneuil Hall, into the North End, across the Charlestown Bridge, and up to the Bunker Hill Monument. Along the way you pass the oldest public park in the country, the building where the Boston Tea Party was planned, the site of the Boston Massacre, Paul Revere's actual house, the steeple where the two lanterns were hung, and the oldest commissioned warship still afloat.

Two things make it work as a walk rather than a checklist. The first is that the sites are arranged chronologically enough that the story makes sense as you go: colonial Boston downtown, the run-up to the Revolution in the meeting houses, the night of April 18, 1775 in the North End, and the first major battle across the river. The second is that the trail crosses Boston's best neighborhoods on the way. You get Beacon Hill at the start, the North End in the middle and Charlestown at the end, and the walk between stops is as much the point as the stops themselves.

The trail is maintained by the City of Boston, the Freedom Trail Foundation, and the National Park Service, which runs Boston National Historical Park and staffs several of the sites. That is why admission policies vary: the Park Service sites and the churches are free or donation-based, while three sites run by independent nonprofits charge a ticket. If you like to work through a route stop by stop, you can do it as a checklist; otherwise, the list below is all you need.

02

How long the Freedom Trail takes

The Freedom Trail is 2.5 miles, or about 4 kilometers, end to end. If you walked it without stopping at a normal city pace you would finish in roughly an hour, and some people do exactly that as a scenic route to Charlestown. Nobody recommends it. The realistic range is three to five hours, and the difference is entirely about how many interiors you go into and how long you linger in the North End.

A useful way to think about it is in three legs. The Downtown leg, from Boston Common to Faneuil Hall, is about a mile and holds 11 of the 16 stops. It is dense, mostly free, and takes 90 minutes to two hours with the burying grounds and a couple of interiors. The North End leg, from Faneuil Hall to Copp's Hill, is about half a mile with three stops, and takes an hour if you eat, which you should. The Charlestown leg, from Copp's Hill across the bridge to the USS Constitution and up to Bunker Hill, is the last mile, has two stops, and takes 60 to 90 minutes including the monument climb.

Terrain is flat except for two short climbs: up Beacon Street to the State House at the beginning, and up Breed's Hill to the monument at the end. The sidewalks are brick and uneven in places, and Downtown is crowded from late morning on. If you have a stroller or limited mobility, the Downtown and North End legs are fine; the Charlestown Bridge has a good sidewalk but the hill up to the monument is steep, and the 294 steps inside it have no elevator.

03

All 16 Freedom Trail stops, in order

Listed in the order you meet them walking from Boston Common to Charlestown. "Free" means you can enter or view the site at no cost; where a site charges, the price is what was posted in September 2026 and is worth checking before you go.

1. Boston Common

139 Tremont St (Visitor Information Center)Park Street (Red/Green)Park always open; center daily 9–5Free

Boston Common was bought by the town in 1634 from William Blaxton, the first European settler on the peninsula, and is the oldest public park in the United States. It has been a cow pasture, a militia training ground, a British army camp during the occupation of 1775, a site of public hangings, and the place where Martin Luther King Jr. spoke to 22,000 people in 1965. The trail officially begins at the Visitor Information Center on the Tremont Street side, where you can pick up a free map.

Local tip: the Public Garden across Charles Street is not on the trail but is a five-minute detour and prettier than the Common. Do it first, before you start, rather than doubling back later.

2. Massachusetts State House

24 Beacon StPark Street (Red/Green)Mon–Fri 8:45–5, free tours 10–3:30Free

The gold-domed State House at the top of the Common was designed by Charles Bulfinch and finished in 1798 on land that had been John Hancock's cow pasture. The dome was originally wood shingle, then copper laid by Paul Revere's company, and has been gilded in 23-karat gold leaf since 1874. Inside are the House and Senate chambers, the Hall of Flags, and the Sacred Cod, a carved wooden fish that has hung in the House chamber since 1784. Free guided tours run on weekdays; you can also walk through on your own.

3. Park Street Church

1 Park StPark Street (Red/Green)Interior open summer weekdays; check before you goFree

Park Street Church was built in 1809 on the site of the town granary, and its 217-foot steeple was the first thing people saw approaching Boston by land for most of the 19th century. It is best known for what was said in it: William Lloyd Garrison gave his first major anti-slavery speech here in 1829, and "America" ("My Country, 'Tis of Thee") was first sung on its steps in 1831. The corner outside was once called Brimstone Corner, either for the sermons or for the gunpowder stored in the basement during the War of 1812.

4. Granary Burying Ground

Tremont St, next to Park Street ChurchPark Street (Red/Green)Daily, roughly 9–5Free

The Granary, founded in 1660, is the third-oldest burying ground in Boston and by some distance the most visited, because of who is in it. Samuel Adams, John Hancock, Paul Revere, James Otis, Peter Faneuil, the five victims of the Boston Massacre, and Benjamin Franklin's parents are all here, under about 2,300 markers for what is thought to be more than 5,000 burials. The slate headstones with winged skulls and cherubs are some of the best surviving colonial stone carving in the country.

Local tip: the large obelisk in the center is the Franklin family marker, not Ben Franklin himself, who is buried in Philadelphia. Hancock's monument is on the left wall as you enter; Revere's is at the back.

5. King's Chapel and King's Chapel Burying Ground

58 Tremont StPark Street or Government CenterChapel open most days, donation suggested; burying ground dailyFree (donation)

King's Chapel was the first Anglican church in New England, founded in 1686 at the order of a governor the Puritans despised. Nobody would sell the Crown land for it, so it was built on the corner of the town's oldest burying ground. The current stone building dates to 1754 and was designed to have a steeple that was never built, which is why it looks unfinished. The burying ground next door, from 1630, holds John Winthrop, the colony's first governor, and Mary Chilton, said to be the first woman off the Mayflower.

6. Boston Latin School site and Benjamin Franklin statue

School St, outside Old City HallGovernment Center or State (Blue/Orange)Public sidewalk, always openFree

A mosaic in the sidewalk on School Street marks the site of Boston Latin School, founded in 1635 and the oldest public school in the United States. It still exists, now in Fenway. Its dropouts include Benjamin Franklin, whose statue stands in the courtyard of Old City Hall behind the mosaic; its graduates include Samuel Adams, John Hancock and Cotton Mather. The Old City Hall itself, a French Second Empire building from 1865, now holds offices and a steakhouse, and is worth a look from the courtyard.

7. Old Corner Bookstore

283 Washington St, corner of School StState (Blue/Orange) or Downtown CrossingExterior onlyFree

The brick building on the corner of School and Washington Streets dates to 1718 and is one of the oldest commercial buildings in the city. From 1832 to 1865 it housed the publisher Ticknor and Fields, which put out Thoreau's Walden, Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter, Longfellow's Paul Revere's Ride and the Atlantic Monthly, and made this corner the center of American literary life for a generation. It is now a fast-food restaurant, which locals have made their peace with; the building survives because Historic Boston Incorporated bought it in 1960 to stop it being demolished.

8. Old South Meeting House

310 Washington StDowntown Crossing (Red/Orange) or StateDaily, roughly 10–5; check seasonal hoursPaid: about $15 adults, combined ticket with Old State House

On December 16, 1773, more than 5,000 people packed into this meeting house to argue about three ships of tea sitting in the harbor. When the meeting broke up, Samuel Adams gave what many believe was the signal, and a group of men headed for Griffin's Wharf to dump 342 chests of tea into the water. Built in 1729 as a Puritan meeting house, it was the largest building in colonial Boston and hosted most of the big protest meetings of the 1760s and 70s. The British used it as a riding school during the occupation. It is run by Revolutionary Spaces, the same organization that runs the Old State House, and one ticket covers both.

9. Old State House

206 Washington StState (Blue/Orange), directly underneathDaily, roughly 10–5; check seasonal hoursPaid: about $15 adults, combined with Old South Meeting House

The Old State House, built in 1713, is the oldest surviving public building in Boston and was the seat of colonial government, the place where James Otis argued against the writs of assistance in 1761 (the speech John Adams said gave birth to the child of independence), and the building from whose balcony the Declaration of Independence was first read to Bostonians on July 18, 1776. The lion and unicorn on the roof are replicas; the originals were burned that day. It has been a museum since 1881, and the subway runs directly beneath it, which is a very Boston arrangement.

10. Boston Massacre site

Cobblestone circle in the traffic island in front of the Old State House, Congress and State StsState (Blue/Orange)Public, always openFree

A ring of cobblestones in the pavement below the Old State House balcony marks the spot where, on March 5, 1770, British soldiers fired into a crowd and killed five men, including Crispus Attucks, a sailor of African and Native American descent who is often called the first casualty of the Revolution. The soldiers were defended at trial by John Adams and most were acquitted, which Adams later called one of the best things he ever did for his country. The marker is small and easy to miss; look for it in the traffic island rather than the sidewalk.

11. Faneuil Hall

4 S Market St (Dock Square)Government Center (Blue/Green) or StateGreat Hall daily 9–5 when not in use; NPS visitor center insideFree

Peter Faneuil, a merchant, gave Boston this market and meeting hall in 1742, and its Great Hall upstairs is where Samuel Adams and James Otis argued against the Stamp Act and the Townshend duties, which is why it is called the Cradle of Liberty. It was enlarged by Charles Bulfinch in 1806 and is still a working meeting hall. The ground floor is shops, the second-floor Great Hall is free to visit with a Park Service ranger on hand, and the National Park Service visitor center on the first floor has bathrooms, maps and the free ranger walk schedule. Quincy Market and the two flanking market buildings behind it are the food halls.

12. Paul Revere House

19 North Square, North EndHaymarket (Orange/Green), 8-minute walkDaily in season; closed Mondays Jan–Mar; check before you goPaid: about $6 adults

Built around 1680, the Paul Revere House is the oldest house in downtown Boston and one of the few surviving 17th-century buildings in any American city. Revere bought it in 1770 and left from here on the night of April 18, 1775. He was a silversmith, engraver, dentist, foundry owner and father of 16, and the house is small enough that the visit takes about 20 minutes, which is about right. The cobbled North Square outside, with the Pierce/Hichborn House next door, is one of the most complete colonial streetscapes in Boston.

13. Old North Church

193 Salem St, North EndHaymarket (Orange/Green), 10-minute walkDaily, roughly 10–5; check seasonal hoursFree (donation suggested); paid tours of the crypt and bell tower

Christ Church in the City of Boston, better known as the Old North, is the oldest church building in the city, from 1723, and the tallest structure in the North End. On the night of April 18, 1775, sexton Robert Newman climbed the steeple and held up two lanterns to signal that the British were leaving by water across the Back Bay rather than by land over the Neck, the "one if by land, two if by sea" of Longfellow's poem. The nave with its box pews is free to enter; the tours of the crypt beneath and the bell-ringing chamber above are paid and worth it if you have the time. The brick Paul Revere Mall between the church and Hanover Street, with its equestrian statue of Revere, is the classic photo.

14. Copp's Hill Burying Ground

Hull St at Snow Hill St, North EndNorth Station (Orange/Green), 10-minute walkDaily, roughly 9–5Free

Copp's Hill, founded in 1659, is the second-oldest burying ground in Boston and sits on the highest point in the North End, with views across the Charles River mouth to Charlestown and the Navy Yard. Cotton and Increase Mather are here, as are Robert Newman of the lanterns and Prince Hall, the founder of Black Freemasonry. More than a thousand free Black Bostonians from the 18th-century community on the north slope were buried here. The British used the hill as an artillery position during the Battle of Bunker Hill, firing across the river at Charlestown, which you can see from the fence.

Local tip: the narrowest house in Boston, 44 Hull Street, about 10 feet wide, is directly across from the burying ground gate.

15. USS Constitution

Charlestown Navy Yard, Building 5, 1st AveNorth Station, 15-minute walk, or the Long Wharf ferryTue–Sun roughly 10–6 in season; check hoursFree; photo ID required; museum by donation

Launched in 1797 from a shipyard in the North End, the USS Constitution is the oldest commissioned warship afloat in the world and is still crewed by active-duty US Navy sailors, who give the tours. It earned the name Old Ironsides in 1812 when British cannonballs bounced off its live-oak hull. Boarding is free with a photo ID and a security screening. The USS Constitution Museum next door is separately run, asks for a donation, and is good for kids. The Navy Yard itself, a Park Service site, has a second ship, the destroyer USS Cassin Young, which is also free to board.

16. Bunker Hill Monument

Monument Square, CharlestownCommunity College (Orange), 10-minute walkGrounds always open; climb roughly 10–5, seasonal; check before you goFree

The 221-foot granite obelisk marks the site of the Battle of Bunker Hill on June 17, 1775, which was actually fought on Breed's Hill, where the monument stands. The colonial militia lost the hill but killed or wounded more than a thousand British soldiers, and the battle proved the war would not be short. The monument was finished in 1843, and the 294 steps to the top are free to climb when it is open, with a view over Charlestown, the harbor and the Zakim Bridge from the small windows at the top. The Park Service museum across the square is free and explains the battle better than the monument does.

05

How to do the Freedom Trail in 1 hour, a half day or a full day

One hour. Walk the Downtown leg from Boston Common to Faneuil Hall and stop only at the outdoor sites: the State House steps, the Granary Burying Ground, the King's Chapel Burying Ground, the Old Corner Bookstore, the Boston Massacre marker and the outside of the Old State House. That gets you nine of the 16 stops and the best headstones in the city, and it finishes at Quincy Market, where you can eat. Skip all interiors. If you are on a layover or seeing the city in a day, this is the version to do; our one-day Boston itinerary builds around it.

Half a day (3–4 hours). Do the full Downtown leg with the Granary, King's Chapel and Faneuil Hall's Great Hall inside, choose one of the two paid Revolutionary Spaces sites (the Old State House is the better building; the Old South Meeting House is the better story), then carry on to the North End for the Paul Revere House, the Old North Church and Copp's Hill. Eat on Hanover or Salem Street. Stop there. The Charlestown leg is worth a separate visit, or you can take the ferry from Long Wharf to the Navy Yard on another morning.

A full day (5–6 hours). Start at the Common at 9am, when the burying grounds and the State House open. Go inside the State House, both Revolutionary Spaces sites and Faneuil Hall. Reach the North End by 12:30, eat, then do the Revere House and the Old North Church including the crypt tour. Cross the bridge to the Navy Yard by 3pm for the Constitution, walk up to the monument, and climb it if it is open. Finish at 4:30 and take the ferry back to Long Wharf, which lands you at the waterfront in time for the harbor at golden hour. That is the whole trail, done properly, in one day, with a harbor crossing to end it.

06

Guided or self-guided: which is better

The honest answer is that the Freedom Trail is designed to be walked without a guide, and this page plus the free map is enough. The red line is impossible to lose for long, every stop has a sign, and most interiors have staff or rangers who will answer questions. If you enjoy reading plaques and setting your own pace, go self-guided.

That said, a guide adds two things: the stories the plaques leave out, and momentum, because a group keeps moving and you will actually reach Charlestown. There are two main options.

National Park Service ranger walks (free). Boston National Historical Park rangers lead free walks along the Downtown section of the trail from the visitor center at Faneuil Hall, typically from spring through fall, several times a day in summer. They are first-come, first-served, about an hour long, and end in the North End. Rangers are knowledgeable and not performing. Check the schedule at the visitor center or on the park's site the morning you go, because times change with staffing and weather. Our free walking tours in Boston guide has the details.

Freedom Trail Foundation tours (paid). The Foundation, the nonprofit that helps maintain the trail, runs the well-known "Walk Into History" tours led by guides in 18th-century costume, playing a specific historical character. They start at the Boston Common Visitor Information Center, run about 90 minutes and cover the Downtown stops to Faneuil Hall. Tickets are in the range of $20 for adults, less for children; check the current price. They are theatrical and good, especially with kids, and run year-round with more departures in summer. There are also specialty tours in the North End and Charlestown, and a pub-crawl version in the evening.

Several other companies run Freedom Trail walks, including free tip-based tours that gather near the Common. The distinguishing thing about the Foundation tours is the costumed character and the fact that the fee supports the trail itself. Whichever you choose, no tour covers all 16 sites; they end at Faneuil Hall or in the North End, and you finish the Charlestown leg on your own.

07

Where to eat along the Freedom Trail

The trail passes the largest concentration of food in Boston, so there is no reason to eat badly. The rule is to hold out for the North End if you can, and use Downtown for coffee.

Quincy Market food hall

Behind Faneuil Hall, 4 S Market StGovernment Center (Blue/Green)Daily, roughly 10–9$10–20

The colonnade inside Quincy Market is a long row of food stalls: clam chowder in bread bowls, lobster rolls, pizza, sandwiches and everything else. It is touristy and the prices reflect it, but it is fast, it is exactly at the halfway point, and eating on the steps outside watching the street performers is a legitimate Boston experience. Boston Chowda and the Boston & Maine Fish Company are the stalls locals would point you to.

Hanover Street and Salem Street, North End

Between stops 11 and 13Haymarket (Orange/Green)Lunch from about 11:30$15–35

The trail runs straight down Hanover Street, the North End's main drag, past dozens of Italian restaurants. For a sit-down lunch, Salem Street a block over is quieter and more local. For something fast, Galleria Umberto at 289 Hanover sells Sicilian pizza squares and arancini for a few dollars until it sells out, usually early afternoon, cash only. For cannoli, Mike's Pastry at 300 Hanover and Modern Pastry at 257 Hanover are the two famous ones and the line at Mike's moves faster than it looks. Full picks in our North End restaurant guide.

Boston Public Market and Haymarket

100 Hanover St, on the GreenwayHaymarket (Orange/Green)Market daily; Haymarket stalls Fri–Sat$5–15

Between Faneuil Hall and the North End, the Boston Public Market is an indoor hall of about 30 local vendors: doughnuts, cider, cheese, dumplings, oysters. On Fridays and Saturdays, the outdoor Haymarket produce stalls set up on Blackstone Street next to it with the cheapest fruit in the city. Both are a two-minute detour from the red line.

Charlestown: Monument Square and Main Street

Around stop 16Community College (Orange)Afternoon and evening$10–30

If you finish in Charlestown hungry, the Warren Tavern at 2 Pleasant Street has been serving since 1780, claims Paul Revere and George Washington as former customers, and does a good burger and chowder. Zume's Coffee House at 223 Main Street is the neighborhood café. Both are a few minutes from the monument.

08

Tips before you walk the Freedom Trail

Start early. Most sites open between 9 and 10am. If you are on the Common at 8:30, you will have the Granary Burying Ground almost to yourself, reach Faneuil Hall before the crowds, and be in the North End when the bakeries are fresh and the lunch lines have not formed. Starting after noon is how people end up skipping Charlestown.

Wear real shoes. The trail is 2.5 miles of brick, cobblestone and uneven sidewalk, plus the State House hill, Copp's Hill and Breed's Hill, and the 294 steps of the monument if you climb it. Sandals are a mistake you will notice by the North End.

Know where the bathrooms are. There are public restrooms at the Boston Common Visitor Information Center, the State House, the National Park Service visitor center inside Faneuil Hall, Quincy Market, the Boston Public Market, the USS Constitution Museum, and the Bunker Hill Museum. The North End stretch between Faneuil Hall and the Navy Yard has none unless you buy something.

Take the ferry back. The trail ends in Charlestown, a mile and a half from where you started. Rather than walking back, go down to the Navy Yard pier and take the MBTA ferry to Long Wharf. It runs roughly every 30 minutes, costs the same as a subway fare, takes about 10 minutes and gives you the skyline from the water. If you have time, walk the Harborwalk from Long Wharf afterward.

Buy the combined ticket if you want both Revolutionary Spaces sites. The Old State House and Old South Meeting House sell one ticket that covers both, and it is cheaper than paying twice. Neither is essential if you are on a tight budget; the trail is very good with only the free sites.

Do the Public Garden first. It is across the street from the start and not on the trail. If you save it for the end you will not go back.

Check seasonal hours in winter. From November to March the Paul Revere House closes on Mondays, the monument climb has shorter hours, and the burying grounds close around 3pm. The walk itself is fine year-round and quiet in the cold, but plan interiors ahead.

Give the North End its own time. The three North End stops are close together and the neighborhood around them is the best part of the walk. If you are not in a hurry, get a coffee, sit in the Paul Revere Mall, and read the plaques on the wall. Then cross the bridge.

FAQ

The Freedom Trail: common questions

How long is the Freedom Trail in Boston?

The Freedom Trail is 2.5 miles (about 4 km) long, from Boston Common to the Bunker Hill Monument in Charlestown. Walking it without stopping takes about an hour; with time inside the sites, plan on three to five hours.

Is the Freedom Trail free?

Walking the Freedom Trail is free, and 13 of the 16 stops are free to enter or view from the outside. Three sites charge admission: the Old State House, the Old South Meeting House and the Paul Revere House. Everything else, including the State House, the burying grounds, the Old North Church nave, Faneuil Hall, the USS Constitution and the Bunker Hill Monument, is free.

Where does the Freedom Trail start and end?

It starts at the Boston Common Visitor Information Center at 139 Tremont Street, next to Park Street station, and ends at the Bunker Hill Monument in Charlestown. You can walk it in either direction, but starting on the Common is easier because the sites are denser at that end.

Do you need a guide for the Freedom Trail?

No. The trail is marked by a red line of brick or paint in the sidewalk and is easy to follow on your own. The National Park Service runs free ranger-led walks in season, and the Freedom Trail Foundation runs paid costumed tours if you want a guide.

Is there a Freedom Trail map?

Yes. Free printed maps are available at the Boston Common Visitor Information Center and the National Park Service visitor center at Faneuil Hall. The stop table in this guide lists all 16 sites in order with addresses; you can follow the red line between them without a map.

How do I get back from Charlestown after the Freedom Trail?

The easiest way is the MBTA ferry from the Charlestown Navy Yard pier to Long Wharf downtown. It costs the same as a subway ride and takes about 10 minutes. Otherwise, walk 15 minutes to North Station (Orange and Green Lines) or Community College (Orange Line).

What is the best time of year to walk the Freedom Trail?

Late April through October is best for weather and for full site hours; September and early October are ideal. The trail is open year-round, but several sites shorten hours or close on some days from November to March, and the Bunker Hill Monument climb is seasonal.
