Things to do in Beacon Hill.
Gas lamps, cobblestones and Charles Street.

By no bad days club, BostonUpdated September 8, 202613 min read

Beacon Hill is the neighborhood people picture when they picture Boston: brick sidewalks that tilt, black shutters, window boxes, purple-tinted glass in the oldest windows, and about a thousand gas lamps that stay lit day and night because nobody has ever installed a way to turn them off. It is a fifth of a square mile between the State House and the Charles River, and every street on it goes uphill in one direction and down in the other.

It is also more than a postcard. The north slope of the hill was the center of Boston's free Black community for most of the 1800s, and the meeting house where William Lloyd Garrison founded the New England Anti-Slavery Society still stands on Joy Street. The State House on top has been the seat of Massachusetts government since 1798, and you can walk in for free. Charles Street at the bottom has the best small run of independent shops and cafés in the city, and the Esplanade is one footbridge away.

This guide covers the best things to do in Beacon Hill, where to eat and shop on Charles Street, the Black Heritage Trail, how the hill was cut down to build the neighborhood, the side streets, the parks on its edges, the year's events and practical tips. It is a ten-minute walk from Downtown and shares the Public Garden with Back Bay, so it fits easily into a day with either.

02

The best things to do in Beacon Hill

Start at the top by the State House and work downhill to Charles Street and the river; the order below roughly follows that walk.

The Massachusetts State House

24 Beacon StRed or Green Line to Park Street, 2-minute walkWeekdays, roughly 8:45am–5pm; free tours roughly 10am–3:30pm, check before you goFree

Charles Bulfinch's 1798 State House is the building that made the hill: the red-brick front with white columns and the dome, gilded in 23-karat gold leaf since 1874, sits on land that was John Hancock's cow pasture. It is a working legislature and it is open to the public. The free 40-minute tours from Doric Hall take in the House chamber and the Sacred Cod, a four-foot carved fish that has hung there since 1784 as a symbol of the fishing industry, the Senate chamber, the Hall of Flags and the marble Memorial Hall. You can also wander the public floors on your own. Bring ID for security. Across Beacon Street, the Robert Gould Shaw and 54th Regiment Memorial, Augustus Saint-Gaudens's bronze relief of the first Black regiment raised in the North during the Civil War, is the starting point of the Black Heritage Trail and one of the finest pieces of public sculpture in the country.

Acorn Street

Between West Cedar St and Willow St, just below Mount Vernon StRed Line to Charles/MGH, 6-minute walk uphillPublic street, always openFree

One block, about 200 feet long, of rough cobblestones between two rows of small brick houses that were built in the 1820s for the coachmen and tradesmen who served the mansions on Mount Vernon Street above. It is now the most photographed street in Boston, which the residents put up with. The stones are original, the lamps are gas, the window boxes are competitive. Go before 8am if you want it to yourself; by mid-morning on a weekend there will be a queue at the top for the shot looking downhill toward the river. It is also genuinely steep and the cobbles are uneven, so mind your footing.

Louisburg Square and Mount Vernon Street

Louisburg Square, between Mount Vernon St and Pinckney StRed Line to Charles/MGH, 7-minute walkThe park is private; the streets around it are publicFree

Boston's most expensive address is a small, fenced, privately owned park surrounded by bowfront brick houses from the 1830s and 40s. Louisa May Alcott died at number 10; a former Secretary of State lives at number 19. You cannot go into the park (the residents own it, and the gates are locked), but the square is the best single view of what the hill's south slope was built to look like. Mount Vernon Street, which runs along its top, is the grandest street on the hill, with Bulfinch's freestanding mansions at numbers 55 and 85 set back behind lawns, which is unusual anywhere in Boston. Henry James called it the only respectable street in America.

The Nichols House Museum

55 Mount Vernon StRed or Green Line to Park Street, 6-minute walkGuided tours several days a week, roughly Thu–Sun; check before you goAround $15

The one house on the hill you can go inside as it was lived in. Built in 1804 to a Bulfinch design, it was the home of Rose Standish Nichols, a landscape gardener, pacifist and suffragist who left it to the public on her death in 1960 with everything in place: Flemish tapestries, family furniture going back to the 1700s, her own carved chairs, and a view over the rooftops toward the river. The tours are small and about an hour, and the guides are good on the daily life of the hill's women, servants and reformers rather than just the furniture.

The Museum of African American History

46 Joy StRed or Green Line to Park Street, 8-minute walk; Blue Line to Bowdoin, 5 minutesRoughly Tue–Sat, 10am–4pm; check before you goAround $10 adults

Two buildings at the top of Joy Street on the north slope: the African Meeting House of 1806, the oldest surviving Black church building in the United States, and the Abiel Smith School of 1835, the first public school in the country built for Black children. Frederick Douglass spoke in the meeting house; Garrison founded the New England Anti-Slavery Society there in 1832; the 54th Massachusetts recruited there. The restored meeting house sanctuary is plain and very moving, and the exhibits in the school next door explain the community that built it. It is the anchor of the Black Heritage Trail, covered in its own section below.

The Vilna Shul

18 Phillips StRed Line to Charles/MGH, 5-minute walkOpen to visitors on set days, with tours; check the schedule before you goSmall admission or donation; check

A 1919 synagogue built by immigrants from Vilnius, and the last surviving immigrant-era synagogue building in downtown Boston. The West End next door once had dozens; urban renewal in the 1950s took all of them. The Vilna Shul closed as a congregation in 1985, sat empty, and has been restored since as a cultural center, with the original folk-painted murals uncovered on the walls, pews rescued from a Baptist church, and a skylit sanctuary that is quiet in the middle of the day. It tells the part of the hill's story that the brick mansions skip.

The Longfellow Bridge

From Charles Circle, at the Charles/MGH station, across to Kendall SquareRed Line to Charles/MGHAlways open; sidewalks on both sidesFree

The 1906 stone-and-steel bridge that carries the Red Line over the Charles, with four granite towers that Bostonians call the salt and pepper shakers. It was rebuilt piece by piece between 2013 and 2018 and the walk across takes ten minutes. From the middle you get the Esplanade and the Back Bay skyline on one side, the Museum of Science and the Zakim Bridge on the other, and the Beacon Hill rooftops behind you. Sunset from the downstream sidewalk is the best free view on this side of the river. The Red Line riding beside you is part of the experience.

Cheers, the Bull & Finch Pub

84 Beacon St, at the Public GardenGreen Line to Arlington, 3-minute walkDaily, lunch until latePub prices; burgers around $20

The Bull & Finch opened in 1969 in the basement of the Hampshire House, and its exterior was used in the opening credits of Cheers from 1982 to 1993. The bar inside was never used for filming and does not look like the set; there is a replica of the TV bar upstairs. It is a real pub, it is always busy with visitors, and the food is what you would expect. Take the photo on the steps, have one beer at the downstairs bar if you are a fan, and go eat somewhere on Charles Street.

03

Charles Street: where to eat and shop in Beacon Hill

Six blocks between the Public Garden and Cambridge Street, with no chains beyond a couple of coffee shops, and the best concentration of small independent shops in the city.

How to walk Charles Street

Start at the Public Garden end by the Cheers steps and walk north; the street tilts gently downhill toward the river and the Charles/MGH station at the bottom. Antique shops were the original trade here and a dozen survive, mostly between Chestnut and Pinckney. Between them: a hardware store that has been here for a century, a florist, two bookstores, a butcher Julia Child used, and enough cafés to make the whole walk take a morning. The cross streets uphill (Chestnut, Mount Vernon, Pinckney, Revere) are the way into the neighborhood proper.

The Paramount: breakfast

44 Charles StRed Line to Charles/MGH, 4-minute walkDaily from early morning; dinner most nights; check before you goBreakfast $12–20

A neighborhood diner since 1937 with a cafeteria-style line for breakfast and lunch: you order at the counter, carry your plate, and the rule is you cannot sit down until you have your food. Pancakes, the omelets, the French toast, and the hash, in a narrow room with a pressed-tin ceiling and the whole hill in line ahead of you on a Sunday. It turns into a table-service restaurant with candles at dinner, which is the quieter way to see it.

Tatte in the Charles Street Meeting House

70 Charles St, at Mount Vernon StRed Line to Charles/MGH, 3-minute walkDaily, early until eveningPastries $5–8; brunch plates $15–22

The Beacon Hill branch of the Boston café chain sits in the ground floor of the 1807 Charles Street Meeting House, a brick church designed by Asher Benjamin that hosted abolitionist speakers in the 1800s and was later an African Methodist Episcopal church. The pistachio croissant, the shakshuka and the halva brioche are the ones to get; the tables outside on the corner are the best people-watching on the street. The best cafés in Boston list has the rest of the city.

Toscano, 75 Chestnut and Grotto: dinner

Toscano, 47 Charles St · 75 Chestnut, 75 Chestnut St · Grotto, 37 Bowdoin StCharles/MGH or Park StreetDinner nightly; reservations for all threeEntrées $25–45

Toscano is the hill's Tuscan standby, with a fireplace, handmade pasta and a bar that has been full of neighbors for thirty years. 75 Chestnut, on a side street, is dark wood and brass, a Sunday jazz brunch and a menu of New England classics that suits the room. Grotto, a basement on the Bowdoin Street side near the State House, is a small Italian room with a three-course prix fixe that is one of the best dinner values in central Boston. For a bigger night, Scampo in the Liberty Hotel at 215 Charles Street is in the old Charles Street Jail, and the hotel's bar, in the jail's central rotunda, is worth a drink whether or not you eat.

The Sevens: the pub

77 Charles StRed Line to Charles/MGH, 3-minute walkDaily, afternoon until latePints around $8; cash preferred, check

The bar locals mean when they say the bar on Charles Street. Dark, narrow, a dartboard, a good pint and a sandwich, and no interest in the fact that Cheers is three blocks away. It is the right place to end a walk on the hill.

The shops: Beacon Hill Books, Blackstone's, Savenor's and the antiques

Beacon Hill Books & Café, 71 Charles St · Blackstone's of Beacon Hill, 46 Charles St · Savenor's Market, 160 Charles St · Rouvalis Flowers, 40 West Cedar StDaytime hours; check before you go

Beacon Hill Books & Café, opened in 2022 in a five-story town house, has a children's floor with a model train running through the walls, a café in the basement and a garden, and is already the best bookstore in central Boston. Blackstone's is where the neighborhood buys gifts and Boston-themed things that are not tacky. Savenor's is the butcher and market where Julia Child shopped when she lived in Cambridge; it still has the sign. The antique shops between Chestnut and Pinckney are worth ducking into even if you are not buying; several have been in the same families for generations. For a longer list see hidden gems in Boston.

The Black Heritage Trail

A 1.6-mile walk through the north slope, where Boston's free Black community lived, organized and hid fugitives from slavery in the decades before the Civil War.

By 1800, most of Boston's roughly 1,000 free Black residents lived on the north slope of Beacon Hill, downhill from the mansions and uphill from the docks and the West End. Over the next sixty years the neighborhood produced a remarkable number of the people who made Boston the center of American abolitionism: ministers, barbers, tailors, sailors and schoolteachers who petitioned, sued, organized, hid fugitives and, when the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 made that a federal crime, kept doing it. The houses on the trail are almost all private homes now; the two you can enter are the meeting house and the school, which together form the Museum of African American History.

The National Park Service runs free ranger-led walks of the trail, usually daily in summer and on a reduced schedule in spring and fall, starting from the Shaw Memorial; check the current times before you go. The self-guided version takes about 90 minutes at a slow pace, and the free trail map is available from the museum or the Boston African American National Historic Site office at 15 State Street. If you only do one thing on the hill beyond the postcard streets, do this.

05

History: three hills to one

Boston was founded in 1630 on a peninsula whose main feature was a ridge of three peaks the settlers called the Trimountaine, the origin of Tremont Street. The middle peak, the highest, carried a warning beacon on a pole from 1634 onward, which gave it its name. For a century and a half the hill was cow pasture, with John Hancock's mansion on the south side facing the Common and, on the north side toward the river, a rough district of taverns and brothels that the British soldiers garrisoned in Boston called Mount Whoredom.

In 1795 a group of investors calling themselves the Mount Vernon Proprietors, with Charles Bulfinch as their architect, bought the pasture and started to develop it, and the State House went up on top in 1798. To make the land buildable, they cut the hill down: the peak was lowered by about sixty feet over the next few decades, using one of the first railways in America, a gravity line that ran carts of earth down to fill in the mill pond to the north. The west peak, Mount Vernon, was removed entirely and dumped into the river to make the Flat of the Hill, which is why Charles Street and everything west of it are level and everything east of it climbs. The east peak, Pemberton Hill, went in 1835 to build Pemberton Square, where the courthouse is now.

What went up on the reshaped hill was the neighborhood you see: Federal-style brick row houses in the 1800s to 1830s, Greek Revival in the 1830s and 40s, mostly designed by Bulfinch and his followers, and mostly built for the merchant families who became the Boston Brahmins. The south slope facing the Common got the big houses, Louisburg Square and Mount Vernon Street. The north slope got smaller houses, tenements and the free Black community, and later the Irish, Jewish and Italian immigrants of the West End below. By the 1880s the wealthy were leaving for the new Back Bay, and for fifty years the hill was faded, subdivided and cheap enough for artists and writers.

The gas lamps are a leftover of that era, not a restoration: they were never removed. Boston has about 2,800 gas lamps, a large share of them on the hill, and they burn continuously because lighting and extinguishing them daily costs more than the gas. The purple windows in a few houses on Beacon and Chestnut Streets are a manufacturing defect: a batch of English glass installed around 1820 turned violet in sunlight because of manganese in the mix, and the panes became a status symbol, so there are imitations too.

In 1955 Beacon Hill became the first historic district in Massachusetts, after residents fought the city over replacing the brick sidewalks, and the rules since then have preserved almost everything, down to the paint colors of the doors. The West End next door, which had none of that protection, was leveled by urban renewal between 1958 and 1960; the Vilna Shul, the Old West Church and the Harrison Gray Otis House on Cambridge Street are about all that remain of it.

06

Side streets and hidden corners of Beacon Hill

Acorn Street and Charles Street take the crowds. Most of the hill is one turn away from being empty.

Chestnut Street

Parallel to Mount Vernon, one street downhill, and the one many residents consider the prettiest on the hill: Bulfinch houses at 13, 15 and 17, purple window panes at 29A, and the Harvard Musical Association at number 57. It is quieter than Mount Vernon and better lit at night. The stretch between Walnut and West Cedar has the best doorways.

Pinckney Street and the ridge

Pinckney runs along the top of the hill and was the dividing line between the south slope's mansions and the north slope's working neighborhood, which you can see in the houses: bigger on the downhill side, plainer uphill. The view down toward the river from the top of the street, framed by houses on both sides, is the classic Beacon Hill photo after Acorn Street. Number 9½ has a hidden house behind it; number 20 was Louisa May Alcott's home before Louisburg Square.

Rollins Place and Cedar Lane Way

Two dead-end lanes off Revere Street and Chestnut Street respectively, and the closest thing the hill has to secret spots. Rollins Place ends in a white Greek Revival portico that is a false front: there is no house behind it, only a drop to the street below, and the facade was built to hide the cliff. Cedar Lane Way is a cobbled alley that runs behind Chestnut and Mount Vernon Streets, wide enough for one car, with gardens, garages and gas lamps and almost nobody in it.

The Sunflower Castle and the Flat

130 Mount Vernon St, at River St

At the bottom of Mount Vernon Street, on the filled land west of Charles Street, is a yellow-and-brown 1840 house remodeled in 1878 in a Queen Anne style with a large sunflower motif on the front. It is a private home and looks like it belongs in Amsterdam. The streets of the Flat around it, Brimmer, Lime, River and Byron, are quieter and flatter than the hill proper, and Byron Street is one of the hidden gems of the neighborhood.

The Boston Athenaeum and the Granary

Boston Athenaeum, 10½ Beacon St · Granary Burying Ground, Tremont St at Park StPark StreetAthenaeum: first floor open to visitors; tours by reservation, check before you go · Granary: daily, free

On the Downtown edge of the hill. The Athenaeum is an 1807 membership library, one of the oldest in the country, in a five-story building overlooking the Granary; the first-floor gallery and reading room are open to visitors and the guided tours go higher. The Granary Burying Ground next door holds Paul Revere, Samuel Adams, John Hancock and the victims of the Boston Massacre, and is on the Freedom Trail.

07

The Common, the Public Garden and the river

Beacon Hill is bordered on two sides by parks and on a third by the river, and a visit usually spills into all three. Boston Common, along Beacon Street below the State House, is the oldest public park in the country (1634), with the Frog Pond (spray pool in summer, skating rink from about November to March), the Parkman Bandstand, and the Brewer Fountain by Park Street station. The Public Garden, across Charles Street from the Common, is the country's first public botanical garden (1837), with the lagoon, the swan boats from mid-April to early September, the weeping willows, and the Make Way for Ducklings bronze sculptures near the Beacon and Charles Street corner, which is the best-loved piece of public art in the city. Cheers is on the corner across the street.

On the river side, the Charles/MGH station sits on an elevated platform at Charles Circle, and the pedestrian bridge from it, or the footbridges just north and south, cross Storrow Drive to the Esplanade. The stretch of the Esplanade closest to the hill has the docks of Community Boating (sailing and kayak rentals in season), the Hatch Shell five minutes south, and the Longfellow Bridge right there. Massachusetts General Hospital takes up most of the land between Cambridge Street and the river; its 1821 Bulfinch Building holds the Ether Dome, the operating theater where anesthesia was first publicly demonstrated in 1846, which is open to visitors when not in use. Check at the hospital's information desk before you go.

For a longer loop, the best walks in Boston page has a Beacon Hill, Esplanade and Back Bay route that takes about two hours.

08

Beacon Hill events through the year

The two events the neighborhood is known for are small and specific. The Beacon Hill Hidden Gardens Tour, run by the Beacon Hill Garden Club on the third Thursday in May, opens a dozen or so private walled gardens behind the houses for one day; it is ticketed, sells out, and is the only way to see the backs of the hill. Halloween on Beacon Hill is a citywide institution: Chestnut, West Cedar, Mount Vernon and the surrounding streets are closed to traffic, the houses go all out on decorations, and thousands of children and adults fill the streets from late afternoon. If you are in Boston on October 31, this is where to be.

December is the other season. The whole hill decorates, with wreaths on nearly every door and lights in the windows, and on Christmas Eve the neighborhood's bell ringers and carolers walk from Louisburg Square through the streets, a tradition that goes back to the 1920s. The Boston Common tree lighting, in late November or early December, and the Frog Pond rink bring the crowds to the edge. In spring, the swan boats return to the Public Garden in mid-April and the Duckling Day parade on Mother's Day sends several hundred children dressed as ducklings from the Common to the Garden. The Beacon Hill Art Walk on the first Sunday in June puts local artists in the courtyards and alleys of the north slope. Our things to do this weekend page lists current dates.

09

Tips for a first visit to Beacon Hill

Go early. Before 9am the streets are empty, the light is good and Acorn Street is yours. By noon on a weekend it is a queue.

Wear real shoes. The sidewalks are brick, uneven and steep, the cobbles are rough, and there is ice on the slopes for three months of the year. Heels and the hill do not get along.

Do the State House on a weekday. It is closed weekends. The free tours are worth the time and the Sacred Cod is a better story than you expect.

Walk downhill. Start at Park Street and the State House and work down to Charles Street and the river. Uphill is the same walk with more complaining.

Remember people live here. Every one of the photogenic doorways is somebody's front door. Stay on the sidewalk, keep off the steps, and skip the flash at night.

Do the north slope too. The Black Heritage Trail and the Vilna Shul are the most substantial history on the hill, and most visitors never cross Pinckney Street to find them.

Skip the Cheers dinner. Take the photo and eat on Charles Street. The Paramount, Tatte, Toscano and the Sevens are all within four blocks.

End at the river. The Longfellow Bridge at sunset, or the Esplanade docks, is the right way to finish. The Boston neighborhoods hub has the rest of the city from there.

FAQ

Beacon Hill Boston: common questions

What is Beacon Hill in Boston known for?

Boston's best-preserved historic neighborhood: steep brick streets, Federal-style row houses from the early 1800s, working gas lamps, the cobblestones of Acorn Street, the gold-domed State House, Louisburg Square, and the antique shops and cafés of Charles Street. It was also the center of Boston's free Black community in the 1800s, which the Black Heritage Trail follows.

How do you get to Beacon Hill?

Park Street (Red and Green Lines) puts you at the foot of the State House; Charles/MGH (Red Line) puts you at the bottom of Charles Street; Arlington (Green Line) is a short walk across the Public Garden to the Charles Street end; Bowdoin (Blue Line) is behind the State House. From Downtown Crossing or Faneuil Hall it is a 10-minute walk. Do not drive; there is almost no parking.

Where is Acorn Street and can you walk on it?

Acorn Street is one block long, running between West Cedar Street and Willow Street just below Mount Vernon Street. It is a public street and you can walk it, but it is residential, so keep voices down, stay off doorsteps, and expect other people taking photographs. Early morning is the only time it is empty.

Is the State House free to visit?

Yes. The Massachusetts State House at 24 Beacon Street is open to the public on weekdays, and free guided tours run roughly 10am to 3:30pm from the Doric Hall entrance; check the schedule before you go, as sessions and events sometimes close rooms. You can also walk through on your own. Bring photo ID for security.

How long do you need for Beacon Hill?

Ninety minutes covers a loop of Charles Street, Acorn Street, Louisburg Square and the State House exterior. Half a day adds the State House tour, the Museum of African American History or a house museum, and lunch on Charles Street. It combines easily with the Public Garden, Boston Common and the Esplanade.

Is the Cheers bar in Beacon Hill the one from the show?

Partly. The exterior of the Bull & Finch Pub at 84 Beacon Street was used for the show's opening credits, but the interior was a studio set in Los Angeles and looks nothing like the bar downstairs. There is a replica of the set bar inside, upstairs. It is a real pub with average food and a lot of visitors; go for the photo, not the dinner.
