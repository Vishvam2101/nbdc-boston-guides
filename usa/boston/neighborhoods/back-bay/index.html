Things to do in Back Bay Boston.
Brownstones, the library and the river.

The Boston Public Library, Copley Square, Newbury Street, the Commonwealth Avenue Mall, View Boston, the Esplanade and the Marathon finish line, with where to eat and how the whole neighborhood was built on a swamp.

Free to enterBates Hall, Boston Public Library
The skylinePrudential and Hancock towers
Filled land since 1857

By no bad days club, BostonUpdated September 8, 202613 min read

Every one of Back Bay's brownstones is standing on gravel that arrived by train from Needham, nine miles west, one carload every 45 minutes, day and night, for more than thirty years. Before 1857 the neighborhood was a tidal marsh behind a mill dam that smelled bad enough at low tide to be a public health issue. What Boston built on top of it is the most complete Victorian neighborhood in the United States: a flat, Parisian grid of tree-lined boulevards and bowfront row houses, with alphabetical cross streets from Arlington to Hereford so that you always know where you are.

For a visitor it is the neighborhood that has everything within eight blocks. The Boston Public Library, the one building in the city everyone should walk into, is on Copley Square, across from Trinity Church and under the Hancock Tower. Newbury Street is a block north. The Charles River Esplanade is two blocks beyond that. The Prudential Tower's observation deck, the Marathon finish line, the Mapparium and half the city's best restaurants are all inside the same walk.

This guide covers the best things to do in Back Bay Boston, where to eat and get coffee, Newbury Street and the shopping, how the neighborhood was built, the quieter streets a block off the main ones, the year's events and practical tips. It sits between the Beacon Hill and Public Garden side of Downtown and the ballpark end of Fenway–Kenmore, and it is the easiest neighborhood in Boston to combine with either.

Back Bay station on Dartmouth Street, a block south of Copley Square, is the neighborhood's rail hub: the Orange Line, all the commuter rail lines that run into South Station, and Amtrak's Northeast Regional and Acela. If you are arriving from New York or Providence by train, get off here rather than at South Station; you are already in the neighborhood. The station's main hall, a barrel-vaulted timber room from 1987, is the least attractive thing about it, but the exits put you straight onto Dartmouth Street.

02

The best things to do in Back Bay

Most of these are free, most are within ten minutes of Copley Square, and the order roughly follows a good day on foot.

The Boston Public Library

700 Boylston St, Copley SquareGreen Line to CopleyMon–Thu 9am–8pm, Fri–Sat 9am–5pm, Sun 1–5pm; check before you goFree

The first large free municipal library in the country, and the one public building in Boston you should walk into even if you have no interest in books. The 1895 McKim building on Dartmouth Street is a Renaissance palace: a marble staircase guarded by lions, murals by John Singer Sargent and Puvis de Chavannes, and Bates Hall, a 218-foot reading room under a coffered barrel vault with green-shaded lamps on long oak tables. Anyone can sit and work there. The arcaded courtyard in the middle of the building, with a fountain and café tables, is the best quiet spot in the neighborhood on a warm day.

Free hour-long art and architecture tours run most days from the Dartmouth Street lobby; check the schedule. The 1972 Johnson building on Boylston Street, renovated in 2016, is the working library, with a café and the Newsfeed area facing the street. Go in the Dartmouth Street door first.

Copley Square and Trinity Church

Trinity Church, 206 Clarendon StGreen Line to CopleyChurch open to visitors most days; self-guided admission around $10, check before you goThe square is free

Copley Square is the best public square in Boston, and the reason is the architecture around it: the library on the west, Trinity Church on the east, the Old South Church (1875, Venetian Gothic, striped stone, free to enter) on the north corner, and the 60-story mirrored John Hancock Tower (1976, I.M. Pei's firm) rising behind the church and reflecting it. Trinity, finished in 1877, is H.H. Richardson's masterpiece and the building that gave the Richardsonian Romanesque style its name: heavy granite and sandstone, a squat central tower, and an interior covered in John La Farge murals and stained glass, with windows by Edward Burne-Jones and William Morris. The paid self-guided visit is worth it for the interior; the square, the fountain and the farmers market on Tuesdays and Fridays from May through November are free.

The Commonwealth Avenue Mall

Commonwealth Ave from Arlington St to Kenmore SquareGreen Line to Arlington, Copley or HynesAlways openFree

A 100-foot-wide, mile-long strip of elms and lawn down the middle of Commonwealth Avenue, laid out in the 1860s on the model of a Paris boulevard and later folded into Frederick Law Olmsted's Emerald Necklace as the link between the Public Garden and the Fenway. Each block has a statue (Alexander Hamilton, William Lloyd Garrison, the Boston Women's Memorial, Samuel Eliot Morison in a slicker on a rock) and the grandest houses in the neighborhood face it: the Ames-Webster mansion at Dartmouth, the Burrage mansion at Hereford, the Hotel Vendome at Dartmouth. In the last week of April and first week of May the magnolias on the sunny north side of the avenue bloom all at once, and the Mall is briefly the most photographed street in the city.

View Boston at the Prudential Tower

800 Boylston St, Prudential Center; entrance on the plaza levelGreen Line to Prudential or HynesDaily, roughly 10am–10pm; check before you goAround $35 adults

The old Skywalk Observatory on the 50th floor closed for good in 2020. What replaced it in 2023 is better: View Boston occupies the top three floors of the Prudential Tower, with a glass-walled 360-degree deck on the 52nd, an open-air roof terrace called Stratus with a bar, and a lounge on the 50th. It is the only high observation deck open to the public in the city now that the Hancock's is long gone, and the view takes in Fenway Park, the river, the Common, the harbor and the whole Back Bay grid below you. Sunset tickets go first. If you would rather have the view with dinner, the Top of the Hub restaurant that used to be up here closed in 2020 and has not been replaced; the roof terrace bar is the alternative. For the free options see the best views in Boston.

The Charles River Esplanade and the Hatch Shell

Along the river from the Longfellow Bridge to the BU Bridge; Hatch Shell at 47 David G. Mugar WayGreen Line to Arlington, then the Arthur Fiedler footbridge; or the Dartmouth St footbridgeAlways openFree

Back Bay's northern edge is a three-mile riverside park with lagoons, a bike path, playgrounds, docks you can sit on, and the Hatch Memorial Shell, where the Boston Pops play on the Fourth of July and free concerts and movies run all summer. Getting there means crossing Storrow Drive on one of the footbridges: the Fiedler bridge at Arlington Street drops you near the Shell, the Dartmouth Street bridge lands you by the lagoon, and the Mass Ave bridge at the far end has the classic skyline photo. Community Boating near the Longfellow Bridge rents sailboats and kayaks in season, and the river is calm enough for beginners. The stretch between the Fiedler bridge and the Mass Ave bridge, at sunset, is one of the great free walks in the city.

The Boston Marathon finish line

Boylston St, in front of the Boston Public LibraryGreen Line to CopleyAlways thereFree

The finish line is painted across Boylston Street year-round, a blue and yellow stripe between the library and the Old South Church, and on any given day you will see someone standing on it having their photo taken. The race, run since 1897, finishes here on Patriots' Day, the third Monday in April, after the last stretch down Boylston from Hereford Street. The two memorials to the 2013 bombing victims, bronze and granite pillars at the two blast sites on the north sidewalk near 671 and 755 Boylston, were installed in 2019 and are worth a quiet minute.

The Mapparium

Mary Baker Eddy Library, 200 Massachusetts AveGreen Line E to Symphony, or HynesTue–Sun, roughly 10am–4pm; check before you goAround $6–8

A three-story stained-glass globe from 1935 that you walk through the middle of on a glass bridge. The world is shown from the inside, so the continents are the right way round but you see them all at once, with the borders as they were in 1935 (French Indochina, Persia, a very large Soviet Union). The acoustics are strange: whisper at one end of the bridge and someone at the other end hears you perfectly. The 20-minute guided visit is the only way in. The building is part of the Christian Science Plaza, whose 670-foot reflecting pool and fountain outside are free and one of the best open spaces in the neighborhood.

The Gibson House Museum

137 Beacon StGreen Line to ArlingtonGuided tours Wed–Sun afternoons; check before you goAround $15

To see what the brownstones look like inside, this is the one: an 1860 Beacon Street row house whose last owner, Charles Gibson Jr., preserved everything exactly as his grandmother had it, down to the Victorian wallpaper, the gas fixtures and the servants' quarters. It has been open as a museum since 1957 and is close to untouched. Tours are small and take an hour.

03

Where to eat and drink in Back Bay

Back Bay has expensive restaurants and expense-account restaurants. These are the ones locals would actually choose, plus the cafés that make the neighborhood livable.

Saltie Girl and Select Oyster Bar: the seafood

Saltie Girl, 281 Dartmouth St · Select Oyster Bar, 50 Gloucester StGreen Line to Copley or HynesLunch and dinner; both small, reservations recommendedLobster roll around $40 at Saltie; entrées $30–50

Saltie Girl is a tiny room on Dartmouth Street with a wall of tinned fish, a torched salmon-belly roll and a fried lobster and waffles dish that gets more attention than the excellent plain lobster roll. Select Oyster Bar, on a side street off Newbury, is quieter and more Mediterranean, with crudo, a good raw bar and a patio. Either is a better bet than the big-name seafood houses on Boylston.

Grill 23: the steakhouse

161 Berkeley StGreen Line to Arlington, or Orange Line to Back BayDinner nightly; reservationsSteaks $60–90

In the 1920s Salada Tea building with bronze doors and a two-story dining room with columns, Grill 23 has been Boston's steakhouse since 1983. Locally owned, dry-aged beef, a wine list that runs to pages, and a bar downstairs where you can get the same steak without the reservation. If you are going to do one expense-account dinner in Boston, this is the one with the least chain feel.

Parish Café: the sandwich lunch

361 Boylston StGreen Line to ArlingtonDaily, lunch until late; check before you goSandwiches $16–22

A bar on Boylston whose menu is sandwiches designed by Boston's well-known chefs, each named for its creator and unchanged for years. It is the answer to lunch near the Public Garden, with a patio on the sidewalk and a full bar until late.

Tatte, Trident and the cafés

Tatte, 399 Boylston St · Trident Booksellers & Café, 338 Newbury St · Flour, 131 Clarendon St · Pavement Coffeehouse, 1096 Boylston StAll within a 10-minute walk of Copley

Tatte's Boylston Street location is the busiest of the Boston chain, with the tiled floors, the shakshuka and the pistachio croissants that made it famous; it is usually full by 10am on weekends. Trident at the Mass Ave end of Newbury has been an independent bookstore with a full café since 1984 and is the best place in the neighborhood to sit for two hours with a book and a plate of eggs. Flour on Clarendon is the Back Bay branch of Joanne Chang's bakery, and Pavement near Berklee is where the music students get their coffee. The full ranking is in the best cafés in Boston.

Contessa and the Oak Long Bar: a drink with a view or a ceiling

Contessa, the Newbury Boston, 1 Newbury St · Oak Long Bar, Fairmont Copley Plaza, 138 St. James AveGreen Line to Arlington or CopleyEvenings; Contessa books out weeks aheadCocktails $18–22

Contessa is the glass-roofed Italian restaurant and bar on top of the Newbury hotel, looking straight down over the Public Garden; a bar seat for a drink is the easiest way in. The Oak Long Bar in the Fairmont Copley Plaza is the opposite: a long, dark, gilded 1912 hotel bar with a painted ceiling and a fireplace, and the right place for a drink on a winter afternoon. The rest of the city's rooftops are in the best rooftop bars in Boston.

Eataly and Bukowski Tavern: the fallbacks

Eataly, 800 Boylston St, Prudential Center · Bukowski Tavern, 50 Dalton StGreen Line to Prudential or Hynes

Eataly in the Prudential Center is three floors of Italian market, counters and restaurants, and a reliable answer when you have a group, a rainy day or no reservation. Bukowski Tavern, wedged under a parking garage on Dalton Street, is the neighborhood's one true dive: a hundred beers, a burger and a wheel you spin to pick a mystery beer. It is where Back Bay goes when it is tired of Back Bay.

Newbury Street and shopping in Back Bay

Eight blocks of brownstones converted to shops, with a clear gradient from designer at the Public Garden end to student at the Mass Ave end.

How to walk Newbury Street

Start at the Public Garden end and walk west; the street gets younger, cheaper and more interesting as you go. The shops are in the ground floors and basements of 1870s brownstones, so the good stuff is often below sidewalk level or up a flight of stairs, and the side streets between Newbury and Commonwealth have galleries and salons that never make the lists. On several Sundays from July through October the city closes the whole street to cars for Open Newbury Street, which is the best time to see it. On a hot Saturday it is the busiest sidewalk in New England.

Trident Booksellers and Café

338 Newbury StGreen Line to HynesDaily, early until late; check before you go

The one shop on Newbury nobody argues about. Two floors of new books, a café that serves breakfast all day, a full bar, and evening events most weeks. It has been here since 1984 and survived a fire in 2019. Go upstairs for the quieter tables by the window.

The Prudential Center and Copley Place

800 Boylston St and 100 Huntington Ave, connected by a covered walkway over HuntingtonGreen Line to Prudential or Copley; Orange Line to Back Bay

Two indoor malls joined by a glass bridge, together making an enclosed route from Boylston Street to Back Bay station that Bostonians use as a shortcut in winter. The Prudential has Eataly, mid-range chains and the entrance to View Boston; Copley Place is the higher end, with Neiman Marcus and the luxury brands that are not on Newbury. Neither is a destination, but on a rainy February day they are the neighborhood's living room. More rainy-day ideas here.

Boylston Street

The commercial spine, with the library, the Marathon finish line and the big flagship stores between Arlington and Mass Ave. The glass Apple store at 815 Boylston is a landmark; so is the 1905 Berkeley Building at 420 Boylston, a white terra-cotta wedding cake that is the prettiest commercial building in the neighborhood. The Boston Architectural College at 320 Newbury has a trompe-l'oeil mural of a building cutaway on its back wall, visible from the Hereford Street side.

05

History: a neighborhood built on water

Until the 1850s, everything west of the Public Garden was the "back bay" of the Charles River: a tidal estuary that filled and emptied twice a day. In 1821 a mill dam was built along the line of today's Beacon Street to power mills with the tide. It failed as a business and succeeded as a nuisance: cut off from the river's flow, the basin behind it became a stagnant flat of sewage and mud that the city's own health board described as offensive to the whole city.

The fix was the largest landfill project in American history to that point. Starting in 1857, the Commonwealth contracted to fill the basin with gravel dug from the hills of Needham and hauled in by a purpose-built railroad. Steam shovels, then new, loaded 35 cars per train; a train arrived roughly every 45 minutes around the clock for years. The fill advanced west from Arlington Street about one block every two years, and the houses followed right behind it, which is why the architecture ages as you walk west: Italianate and French Second Empire near the Garden, Queen Anne and Richardsonian toward Exeter, and the last blocks near Mass Ave finished around 1890 in Colonial Revival. By 1882 the neighborhood had reached Kenmore Square; the Fens beyond were Olmsted's answer to what remained.

The plan was by architect Arthur Gilman, and it was deliberately unlike the rest of Boston. Instead of the medieval tangle of Downtown, Back Bay got a grid: five long east-west streets (Beacon, Marlborough, Commonwealth, Newbury, Boylston), a 240-foot-wide boulevard with a park down the middle, alphabetical cross streets, service alleys behind every block, and strict rules about setbacks and heights. The row houses that went up were the largest and most expensive in the city, and the families who moved in from Beacon Hill made the neighborhood the address of the Boston Brahmin class for the next fifty years.

The brownstones are mostly not brownstone. The name stuck from New York; Back Bay houses are brick with stone trim, in a dozen Victorian styles, and their most consistent feature is the bowfront bay window. Almost every house sits on wooden pilings driven down through the fill into clay, and those pilings rot if the groundwater drops below them, which is why the neighborhood monitors its water table with a network of observation wells and why an occasional house on Beacon Street needs several million dollars of underpinning.

After 1900 the wealthy moved to the suburbs, the houses were cut into apartments and rooming houses, and by the 1950s parts of the neighborhood were shabby. The Prudential Tower (1964) and the Hancock (1976) went up on the southern edge, and the Back Bay Architectural Commission, created in 1966, has since frozen the exteriors of the residential streets more or less as they were in 1890. What you see today is a Victorian neighborhood in better condition than it has ever been.

06

Side streets and quieter corners in Back Bay

Boylston and Newbury take the crowds. The neighborhood the residents see is two streets north.

Marlborough Street

The quietest of the five long streets, entirely residential, lined with the neighborhood's oldest trees and lit at night by gas lamps. There is not a single shop between Arlington and Mass Ave. It is the best street for a slow walk to look at doorways, iron railings and the bowfronts, and in autumn it is the most colorful.

Beacon Street and the river side

The northernmost street, on the line of the old mill dam, with the Gibson House and a row of grand houses whose back windows look over Storrow Drive to the river. The Fiedler and Dartmouth Street footbridges to the Esplanade start here. At the Arlington end, the 1861 Arlington Street Church across from the Public Garden was the first building in the new neighborhood.

The public alleys

Every block has a service alley behind it, numbered rather than named (Public Alley 421, and so on). They were built for coal deliveries and are now where the neighborhood parks its cars, but they are also where you see the backs of the houses: fire escapes, roof decks, the occasional garden. They are public streets and walking them is allowed.

St. Botolph Street

South of the Pike, between Back Bay station and Symphony Hall, a narrow brick street of smaller 1880s row houses that feels more like the South End than Back Bay, because it half is. It has a few good neighborhood restaurants and is the walking route from Copley to Symphony Hall that avoids Huntington Avenue.

The First Baptist Church corner

110 Commonwealth Ave, at Clarendon St

H.H. Richardson's other Back Bay church, from 1871, with a square tower topped by a frieze of trumpeting angels carved by Bartholdi, the sculptor of the Statue of Liberty. Bostonians have called it the Church of the Holy Bean Blowers for 150 years. The corner of Commonwealth and Clarendon around it is the most complete streetscape in the neighborhood.

The Fenway edge

Past Mass Ave, Back Bay becomes Fenway–Kenmore: Symphony Hall at Mass Ave and Huntington, Berklee's buildings around Boylston and Mass Ave, and Kenmore Square with the Citgo sign and the ballpark ten minutes beyond. On game nights the walk from Copley to Fenway Park along Boylston is a 20-minute procession in red.

07

Back Bay events through the year

The one everybody knows is the Boston Marathon, on Patriots' Day, the third Monday in April. The finish line on Boylston Street draws crowds ten deep from mid-morning until the last runners come in after dark, the streets around Copley are closed for the day, and the neighborhood is either the best or the worst place in the city to be, depending on your tolerance for it. The week before, the race expo takes over the Seaport convention center and the finish-line grandstands go up on Boylston.

Late April into May is magnolia season on the Commonwealth Avenue Mall and Marlborough Street, followed by the return of the Copley Square farmers market in May. Summer is the Esplanade: the Boston Pops on the Fourth of July with fireworks over the river (arrive by mid-afternoon for a spot near the Hatch Shell), free Wednesday-night movies at the Shell, and free concerts most weeks. Open Newbury Street car-free Sundays run from July into October. The Boston Pride parade steps off from Copley Square in June.

In the fall, the Boston Book Festival fills Copley Square and the library for a weekend in October, and the Head of the Charles regatta on the third weekend of October is best watched from the Cambridge side, but the Esplanade is a fine second choice. The Prudential Center and Copley Square each light a tree in late November or early December, and First Night on December 31 brings ice sculptures and a parade to Copley. Tips for a first visit to Back Bay

Start at the library. Go in the Dartmouth Street entrance, climb the stairs, sit in Bates Hall for ten minutes, then go out through the courtyard. It sets the tone for the neighborhood and costs nothing.

Walk Newbury west and Commonwealth back east. The two streets are a block apart and completely different. Doing them as a loop from the Public Garden takes about an hour and shows you the whole neighborhood.

Use the alphabet. Arlington, Berkeley, Clarendon, Dartmouth, Exeter, Fairfield, Gloucester, Hereford. If you know the letter you know how far you are from the Garden.

Book View Boston for the last hour of daylight. Sunset slots sell out first; the price is the same at noon, but the view is not.

Cross to the river. Storrow Drive makes the Esplanade feel farther than it is. The footbridges at Arlington, Dartmouth and Mass Ave each take two minutes.

Avoid the big seafood houses on Boylston. They are fine and they are busy, but the Dartmouth and Gloucester Street places are better for the same money.

Marathon Monday is not a regular day. If you want to see the finish line without a crowd, come any other day of the year. If you want the crowd, arrive early and bring patience.

Combine it. Back Bay is ten minutes from Beacon Hill across the Public Garden, ten from the South End across the Pike, and fifteen from Fenway Park. The full route is in our 3-day Boston itinerary.

FAQ

Back Bay Boston: common questions

What is Back Bay in Boston known for?

Boston's Victorian showpiece: a grid of brownstone row houses built on filled tidal marsh between 1857 and about 1890. It is known for the Boston Public Library and Copley Square, Trinity Church, Newbury Street shopping, the Commonwealth Avenue Mall, the Charles River Esplanade, the Prudential and Hancock towers, and the Boston Marathon finish line on Boylston Street.

How do you get to Back Bay?

The Green Line stops at Arlington, Copley and Hynes Convention Center along Boylston Street, and at Prudential on the E branch. Back Bay station on Dartmouth Street has the Orange Line, commuter rail and Amtrak. From Downtown it is a 10-minute walk across the Public Garden. Driving and parking are difficult; take the T.

Is the Prudential Skywalk still open?

No. The Skywalk Observatory on the 50th floor closed permanently in 2020. It was replaced in 2023 by View Boston, a three-floor observation deck on the 50th to 52nd floors of the Prudential Tower with an open-air roof terrace, a bar and a 360-degree view. Tickets are around $35; check hours and prices before you go.

Is Back Bay worth visiting?

Yes. It has the city's best public building in the Boston Public Library, its best square, its best shopping street, its best river walk and its most consistent architecture, and it sits between the Public Garden and Fenway, so it is on the way to almost everything. Half a day covers the highlights; a full day lets you slow down.

What is free to do in Back Bay?

The Boston Public Library, including Bates Hall, the courtyard and the free art and architecture tours; Copley Square and the Old South Church; the Commonwealth Avenue Mall; the Esplanade and the Hatch Shell; window-shopping on Newbury Street; the Marathon finish line; and the Christian Science Plaza reflecting pool. Trinity Church and the Mapparium charge a small admission.

Where should you eat in Back Bay?

For seafood, Saltie Girl on Dartmouth Street or Select Oyster Bar on Gloucester Street. For a steakhouse, Grill 23. For a quick lunch, Parish Café's chef-designed sandwiches on Boylston or Flour on Clarendon. For a café, Tatte on Boylston or the Trident Booksellers café on Newbury. Eataly in the Prudential Center is a reliable fallback.
