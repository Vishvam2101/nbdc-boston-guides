Things to do in the North End.
Boston's Italian neighborhood.

Where to eat, what to see on the Freedom Trail, which side streets to wander, and how to settle the Mike's-versus-Modern cannoli question, with practical tips for a first visit.

By no bad days club, BostonUpdated September 4, 202612 min read

The North End is Boston's oldest residential neighborhood and its Italian district: about 0.36 square miles of narrow streets between the Financial District and the harbor, with nearly a hundred restaurants, cafés and bakeries, three Freedom Trail sites, and a summer calendar of saint's feasts that closes the streets nearly every weekend in July and August.

It is the neighborhood most visitors to Boston end up in, usually hungry, because the Freedom Trail passes through it. It is also the neighborhood locals keep coming back to, because the food is genuinely good, the streets are pleasant to walk at any hour, and it is ten minutes on foot from almost everything else Downtown.

This guide covers the best things to do in the North End, where to eat, the cannoli debate, the historic sites, the quieter side streets, the feasts, and practical tips on cash, reservations and getting there. The restaurant picks are the places people go back to, not just the places they photograph.

02

The best things to do in the North End

A first visit in one list: history in the morning, food in the evening, and the waterfront in between.

Visit the Old North Church

193 Salem StOpen daily; hours vary by seasonAround $5 for the church, more for the crypt tour

Built in 1723, the Old North Church is the oldest surviving church building in Boston and the site of the "one if by land, two if by sea" lantern signal on April 18, 1775. The white steeple is visible from most of the neighborhood. The garden behind the church and the small brick courtyard on the Salem Street side are free and among the quietest spots in the North End.

See Paul Revere's House on North Square

19 North SquareOpen daily, roughly 9:30–5:15 in season$6 adults

Paul Revere's House was built around 1680 and is the oldest surviving house in downtown Boston. Revere lived here from 1770 to 1800, and it was from this house that he left on his ride. The interior takes about 15 minutes. North Square itself, a small cobblestone plaza with the Sacred Heart Church and Mamma Maria on it, is one of the prettiest corners of the neighborhood.

Oysters and a lobster roll at Neptune Oyster

63 Salem StDaily from 11:30amNo reservations; lobster roll around $38

Neptune Oyster is a 40-seat raw bar that many consider the best in Boston, and its hot buttered lobster roll is the one most locals name first. It doesn't take reservations, and the line often starts before it opens. Go at 3pm on a weekday, or go alone and take a bar seat, which comes up much faster than a table.

A pizza at Regina Pizzeria

11½ Thacher StDaily from 11amPizzas $20–30; cash and card

Regina has been on the same corner since 1926, with the original brick oven and wooden booths. The pizza is thin, charred at the edges and slightly greasy in the way North End pizza is supposed to be. There are Regina locations across Boston now, but the Thacher Street original is the one worth the wait.

Christopher Columbus Park and the waterfront

110 Atlantic AveAlways open, free

The park at the southern edge of the neighborhood, on the harbor, is where the North End goes to sit down after dinner. The wisteria trellis blooms in May, there is a fountain and playground in summer, and the harbor and Long Wharf are right in front of you. The Harborwalk continues in both directions, and Langone Park at the north tip has bocce courts and a view of the Zakim Bridge.

03

Where to eat in the North End

Nearly a hundred restaurants, and most are fine. These are the ones that get repeat visits, arranged by what you're looking for.

Giacomo's: the classic no-reservations dinner

355 Hanover StDinner nightly from 4:30pmCash only; entrées $20–30

Giacomo's is the North End restaurant with the line down the block. Big plates of seafood fra diavolo and homemade pasta at prices that have not kept up with the rest of the city. No reservations, cash only, and the wait on a weekend can be an hour. Weeknights before 6pm are the answer.

Galleria Umberto: the best cheap lunch in Boston

289 Hanover StMon–Sat 11am until sold out, usually by 1:30pmCash only; most items under $5

Galleria Umberto has served the same short menu since 1974: Sicilian pizza by the slice, arancini, panzarotti, calzones and a small cup of wine. Everything is under a few dollars and served on paper plates. It closes when the food runs out, so arrive before 1pm.

Mamma Maria: the reservation dinner

3 North SquareDinner nightly; reservations recommendedEntrées $35–50

For the night you want a tablecloth and a quiet room. Mamma Maria is in a townhouse on North Square, with handmade pasta and a Northern Italian menu, and it is one of the few celebrated North End restaurants where a reservation exists and is honored. Bricco on Hanover Street and Prezza on Fleet Street are in the same tier.

Caffè Vittoria: espresso after dinner

290 Hanover StDaily until lateCash only

Caffè Vittoria opened in 1929 as the first Italian café in Boston. Pressed-tin ceilings, antique espresso machines, grappa and a cigar bar downstairs. It is the right place to sit after dinner while the crowd outside lines up for pastry. Caffè Paradiso a block away is the late-night alternative.

Delis and markets: Salumeria Italiana and Polcari's

Salumeria Italiana, 151 Richmond St · Polcari's Coffee, 105 Salem StDaytime hours

If you want to take the North End home, Salumeria Italiana has imported cheeses, cured meats, olive oil and sandwiches to go, and Polcari's has sold coffee, spices and dried goods from wooden barrels since 1932. Both are also good places to see the neighborhood's residents rather than its visitors.

04

Mike's vs Modern vs Bova's: the North End cannoli question

Three bakeries within a few hundred feet of each other, and a rivalry that every Bostonian has an opinion about. Here is how they actually differ.

BakeryAddressKnown forHoursPayment
Modern Pastry257 Hanover StFilled to order, crisp shell, lighter ricotta, smaller and cheaperDaily, until 10–11pmCash and card
Mike's Pastry300 Hanover StLarge cannoli, about 20 flavors, the white box with stringDaily, until 10–11pmCash only
Bova's Bakery134 Salem StOpen 24 hours; also arancini, calzones and sub rolls24 hoursCash and card

Modern Pastry is the purist's choice. The shells are filled when you order, so they stay crisp, and the ricotta filling is lighter and less sweet than Mike's. The cannoli are smaller and cheaper, and the line moves faster. Modern is the one most locals we know pick.

Mike's Pastry is the famous one, and the line outside is part of the experience. The cannoli are enormous, pre-filled, and come in around twenty flavors; the Florentine and pistachio are the ones to get. Mike's is cash only and strict about it.

Bova's Bakery on Salem Street is the one many residents name when asked honestly. It has been open 24 hours a day since 1932, the cannoli are good, and it is the only place in the neighborhood to get an arancini at 2am. Bova's also makes the best spinach calzone in the North End, which gets less attention than it should.

The right answer is to try all three in one walk. It takes about forty minutes, and it is a good excuse to see Salem Street as well as Hanover.

05

Freedom Trail sites and history in the North End

Three of the Freedom Trail's 16 stops are in the North End, and the neighborhood's own history goes back to the 1630s. It has been, in turn, Boston's first suburb, an Irish district, a Jewish district and, since the early 1900s, Italian.

Copp's Hill Burying Ground

Hull St at Snowhill StDaily, roughly 9–5Free

Boston's second-oldest cemetery, established in 1659, on the highest point in the North End. Increase and Cotton Mather are buried here, and British troops fired on Charlestown from the hill during the Battle of Bunker Hill. The view over the harbor to the Charlestown Navy Yard and the USS Constitution is one of the best free views in the neighborhood.

The Skinny House

44 Hull St, across from Copp's HillPrivate residence; exterior only

The narrowest house in Boston, about 10 feet wide, reportedly built out of spite in the 1870s to block a brother's view. It is a private home, so look from the street.

The Great Molasses Flood marker

Langone Park, Commercial StFree

On January 15, 1919, a 2.3-million-gallon molasses tank on Commercial Street burst and sent a wave through the neighborhood, killing 21 people. A small plaque at Langone Park marks the site. The bocce courts and the waterfront are here too.

St. Stephen's Church and the Paul Revere Mall

401 Hanover StFree

St. Stephen's is the only surviving church in Boston designed by Charles Bulfinch, the architect of the State House. Across the street, the Paul Revere Mall (the Prado) is a brick courtyard with the equestrian statue of Revere and a direct view of the Old North Church steeple. It is the most photographed spot in the neighborhood after the bakeries.

06

Side streets and hidden spots in the North End

Hanover Street is where the crowds are. The neighborhood is one block over in every direction.

Salem Street

The residential spine of the neighborhood, parallel to Hanover. Bova's, Neptune Oyster, Polcari's, the good delis and the Old North Church at the end. It is quieter, more local and better for a slow walk than Hanover, and it is where you will see laundry lines and residents on stoops.

Prince Street and the alleys off it

Prince Street runs between Hanover and Salem and is mostly residential. The alleys and courtyards off it are where the neighborhood still looks like it did fifty years ago. The private social clubs along here have no signs and are not open to visitors, but the doorways and window boxes are worth the walk.

All Saints Way

Battery St, off HanoverGate is sometimes open, sometimes not

A narrow alley that one resident has spent decades lining floor to ceiling with images of Catholic saints. It is on private property and the gate is only sometimes open, so seeing inside is a matter of luck. You can see the entrance from the street any time.

The Rose Kennedy Greenway

The park that replaced the elevated highway that cut the North End off from Downtown until 2004. The North End section has fountains, a carousel and food trucks in summer, and it is the pleasant way to walk in from Faneuil Hall or the Aquarium.

07

North End feasts and events

From late June through August, the North End's Italian societies hold religious feasts nearly every weekend. Streets close, a statue of the saint is carried in procession, brass bands play, and the blocks fill with stalls selling sausage and peppers, fried dough, arancini and zeppole. They are free to attend and are the most local thing that happens in the neighborhood all year.

The two biggest are St. Anthony's Feast on the last weekend of August, the largest Italian religious festival in New England, and the Fisherman's Feast in mid-August, which ends with the "flight of the angel" on North Street. Smaller feasts run most other summer weekends; the North End's society calendars are posted on posters around the neighborhood by June.

Outside feast season, the neighborhood is also a good base for the Boston Marathon in April (a 20-minute walk to the finish line on Boylston Street), Fourth of July fireworks (walk to the Harborwalk), and the Head of the Charles in October. Our things to do this weekend page lists what is on.

Tips for a first visit to the North End

Bring cash. Mike's Pastry, Giacomo's, Galleria Umberto, Caffè Vittoria and several others are cash only. There are ATMs on Hanover Street, but the fees are high.

Go on a weeknight. Friday and Saturday evenings mean 30 to 60 minute waits at the no-reservation restaurants and long bakery lines. Tuesday through Thursday, the same places seat you in minutes.

Split the visit. The historic sites are best in the morning, when the streets are empty and the Old North Church has just opened. The restaurants are best in the evening. Many people come twice.

Don't drive. Streets are narrow, many are one way, and resident-only parking covers most of the neighborhood. Take the T to Haymarket or walk from Downtown.

Walk one street over. Salem Street and Prince Street have most of the neighborhood's character and a fraction of Hanover's foot traffic.

Finish on the water. Christopher Columbus Park is a five-minute walk from any restaurant and is the right place to end the evening.

FAQ

North End Boston: common questions

What is the North End in Boston known for?

Italian food, the Freedom Trail and being the city's oldest residential neighborhood. Nearly 100 restaurants and bakeries in under half a square mile, the Old North Church, Paul Revere's House, Copp's Hill, summer feasts and the Mike's-versus-Modern cannoli rivalry.

Mike's Pastry or Modern Pastry: which cannoli is better?

Modern fills to order so the shell stays crisp and the ricotta is lighter. Mike's is larger, sweeter and comes in about twenty flavors. Bova's on Salem Street is open 24 hours and is the local's pick. They are within a few hundred feet of each other, so try all three.

How do you get to the North End?

Orange or Green Line to Haymarket, then a five-minute walk up Hanover Street. Or the Blue Line to Aquarium and a ten-minute walk along the waterfront. It is a ten-minute walk from Faneuil Hall. Don't drive.

How long should you spend in the North End?

Two hours for a walk, the historic sites and a pastry. A full evening for dinner, a café and the waterfront. Many visitors come once in the day and once at night.

Do North End restaurants take reservations?

Many famous ones don't, including Giacomo's, Neptune Oyster and Galleria Umberto, and several are cash only. Mamma Maria, Bricco, Carmelina's and Prezza take reservations. Expect 30 to 60 minute waits at no-reservation places on weekend evenings.

Is the North End safe at night?

Yes. It is one of the busiest and best-lit neighborhoods in Boston in the evening, with restaurants and cafés open late and people on the streets past midnight.

When are the North End feasts?

Most weekends from late June through August. The two largest are the Fisherman's Feast in mid-August and St. Anthony's Feast on the last weekend of August.
