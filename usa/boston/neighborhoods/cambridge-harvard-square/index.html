Things to do in Harvard Square.
The Yard, the museums and the bookstores.

Harvard Yard and its free tours, two of the best museums in the area, five bookstores, a repertory cinema, a folk club, a burger cottage, the river and a cemetery worth the walk, all one Red Line stop from Boston.

Harvard Square is not really a square. It is a triangle of pavement where Massachusetts Avenue, Brattle Street and JFK Street meet above a Red Line station, with the gates of Harvard Yard on one side and a kiosk in the middle that sold newspapers from 1928 until 2019. Around that triangle, within about five minutes' walk, are a free art museum, a case of 4,000 glass flowers, five bookstores, a cinema that has shown Casablanca every year since the 1950s, a basement folk club where Joan Baez started, and a burger place that has been cash-only and closed on Sundays since 1960.

Bostonians have been mourning the square's decline for at least forty years, and it is true that the rents have pushed out a lot of what made it strange. But the university keeps it honest: there are 20,000 students within a mile, the museums are among the best in New England and mostly free, and the street life, chess players, buskers, arguments outside the Coop, is still there. It is the best half-day trip you can take from Boston on the T, and a lot of people who come for the Yard end up staying for dinner.

This guide covers the best things to do in Harvard Square, where to eat and drink, the bookstores, film and music at night, how a 1630 Puritan village became this, the side streets and the walk to the river and to Mount Auburn Cemetery, the year's events, and practical tips. It sits two Red Line stops from Kendall and Central Squares in one direction and two from Davis Square in the other, and the river walk connects it to Allston across the bridge.

Harvard Square quick facts and how to get there

WhereCambridge, across the Charles River from Boston, around the intersection of Massachusetts Avenue, Brattle Street and JFK Street
SizeThe commercial square is a few blocks; with the Yard, the museums and the river it is a 20-minute walk end to end
Nearest T stopHarvard (Red Line), with exits directly into the square, into the Yard's Johnston Gate area and to Church Street. About 10 minutes from Park Street
BusesThe underground busway at Harvard station serves the 1 (to Boston along Mass Ave), 66 (Allston and Brookline), 71 and 73 (Watertown and Belmont, past Mount Auburn Cemetery) and others

Harvard station opened in 1912 as the original terminus of the Red Line and was rebuilt in the 1980s when the line was extended to Alewife. The main entrance is the brick-and-glass headhouse in the middle of the square; the sunken plaza beside it, known for fifty years as the Pit, was the gathering point for skaters, punks and chess players until it was filled in and flattened in 2022 as part of a plaza renovation. The performers and the chess tables have shifted to the flat plaza and to the steps outside the Coop and the Smith Campus Center, and on a warm evening there is usually someone playing.

02

The best things to do in Harvard Square

Campus first, then the museums, then the river; the order is a reasonable day.

Harvard Yard and the free campus tours

Enter at Johnston Gate, Massachusetts Ave opposite the station; tours start at the Smith Campus Center, 1350 Massachusetts AveRed Line to HarvardThe Yard is open all day; tours most days, check the schedule and book aheadFree

The Yard is the oldest part of the oldest university in the country: 25 acres of lawn, elms and brick behind iron fences and a dozen gates, with the 1720 Massachusetts Hall (the oldest building, still a freshman dorm with the president's office on the ground floor), University Hall by Bulfinch, the Memorial Church, and the wide granite steps of Widener Library, which holds about 3.5 million books and is closed to anyone without a Harvard ID. The John Harvard statue in front of University Hall is the most-photographed thing in Cambridge and is known as the statue of three lies: it is not John Harvard (no likeness of him survives, so the sculptor used a student), he did not found the college (he left it his library and half his estate), and the date on the base, 1638, is wrong (1636). Students will tell you not to touch the shoe.

The free student-led tours take about an hour and are the best way to hear the stories; they are run by the university's admissions and visitor office and leave from the Smith Campus Center across Mass Ave. Book ahead in fall and spring. If you would rather wander, walk the Old Yard, cross to the Tercentenary Theatre between Widener and the church, and leave by the gate onto Quincy Street for the museums. On a weekday during term the Yard is full of students walking fast; in summer it is tour groups.

The Harvard Art Museums

32 Quincy StRed Line to Harvard, 5-minute walk through the YardTue–Sun 10am–5pm; closed Mondays; check before you goFree for everyone

Three museums (the Fogg, the Busch-Reisinger and the Arthur M. Sackler) under one glass roof designed by Renzo Piano in 2014, with about 250,000 objects in the collection and a few thousand on view: Italian Renaissance panels, a very strong group of Impressionists and Post-Impressionists including Van Gogh's self-portrait, the best Bauhaus collection outside Germany, Chinese bronzes and jades, and a lot of contemporary work. Admission has been free for everyone since 2023, which makes it the best deal in the region. The building is arranged around a five-story courtyard from the original 1927 Fogg; go up to the top floor to see the conservation labs through the glass, and to the Lightbox gallery under the roof. Ninety minutes is enough for a first visit; it is small enough not to exhaust you and close enough to come back. See also the best museums in Boston.

The Harvard Museum of Natural History and the Glass Flowers

26 Oxford StRed Line to Harvard, 8-minute walkDaily, roughly 9am–5pm; check before you goAround $15 adults; free for Massachusetts residents Sunday mornings, check; ticket includes the Peabody Museum

An old-fashioned university museum in a Victorian brick building north of the Yard, with a great hall of mounted mammals, a room of dinosaurs and a 42-foot kronosaurus, a mineral gallery with a 1,600-pound amethyst geode, and, in a room of their own, the Glass Flowers: about 4,300 botanically exact models of plants in glass, made by Leopold and Rudolf Blaschka, a father and son in Dresden, between 1887 and 1936 for teaching botany. They are not obviously glass. You have to be told, and then look closely at a cross-section of a lily stem or a rotting apple with fungus, and still not quite believe it. The same ticket covers the Peabody Museum of Archaeology and Ethnology connected next door, with Mesoamerican casts, Pacific canoes and a hall of North American material that has been thoughtfully re-hung in recent years.

Mr. Bartley's Burger Cottage

1246 Massachusetts Ave, opposite the YardRed Line to Harvard, 2-minute walkMon–Sat, lunch and dinner; closed Sundays; check before you goBurgers $15–20; cash only, check

Since 1960, a family-run room covered floor to ceiling in signs, posters and bumper stickers, with about thirty seven-ounce burgers named after politicians, celebrities and local jokes, the names changing with the news. The onion rings and the sweet potato fries are as good as the burgers and the frappes (Massachusetts for milkshake) are enormous. There is a line at lunch, the tables are shared, and the family runs it like a diner: order, eat, leave. It has stayed cash-only and Sunday-closed for the entire existence of the businesses around it, which have mostly come and gone.

Longfellow House–Washington's Headquarters

105 Brattle StRed Line to Harvard, 10-minute walk along Brattle StHouse tours late May through October, roughly Wed–Sun; grounds open year-round; check before you goFree

A yellow Georgian mansion from 1759 on Brattle Street that was George Washington's headquarters for the siege of Boston from July 1775 to April 1776, and then the home of Henry Wadsworth Longfellow from 1837 until his death in 1882. It is a National Park Service site, the tours are free and led by rangers, and the house is almost entirely as the Longfellows left it, with the poet's study, his library of 10,000 books and the desk where he wrote Paul Revere's Ride. The formal garden behind it is open whenever the grounds are and is one of the quietest spots near the square. The walk there along Brattle Street, past the row of Loyalist mansions known as Tory Row, is half the point.

The Charles River and the Weeks footbridge

John W. Weeks Bridge, off Memorial Drive at DeWolfe St; JFK Memorial Park at Memorial Dr and JFK StRed Line to Harvard, 8-minute walk down JFK StAlways openFree

Walk down JFK Street from the square and you reach the river in eight minutes at John F. Kennedy Memorial Park, a small green with a fountain inscribed with Kennedy quotations, across Memorial Drive from the Harvard boathouses. Turn left along the river and the 1927 Weeks footbridge, a three-arched brick pedestrian bridge to Harvard Business School, is the best place on the Cambridge bank to watch the rowers, the sunset over the Allston side and, on the third weekend of October, the Head of the Charles regatta, for which it is the most crowded viewpoint on the course. On Sundays from late April to mid-November, Memorial Drive between Western Avenue and Gerry's Landing closes to cars as Riverbend Park, and the whole stretch fills with bikes, runners and strollers. The path continues along the river in both directions for miles; see the best walks in Boston.

Mount Auburn Cemetery

580 Mount Auburn St, CambridgeBus 71 or 73 from the Harvard station busway, 10 minutes; or a 30-minute walk west along Brattle St and Mount Auburn StDaily, roughly 7am–7pm in summer, 5pm in winter; check before you goFree

Not in the square, but the best reason to leave it. Mount Auburn, founded in 1831, was the first garden cemetery in America and the model for every landscaped park that came after it, including Central Park. It is 175 acres of hills, ponds, 5,000 trees and Victorian monuments, and the graves of Longfellow, Mary Baker Eddy, Winslow Homer, Isabella Stewart Gardner, Buckminster Fuller and Bernard Malamud, among thousands of others. Climb the 62-foot Washington Tower in the middle for a view over the trees to the Boston skyline. It is also one of the best birding sites in the state in May. Take the bus out and walk back along Brattle Street past the Longfellow House.

03

Where to eat and drink in Harvard Square

A university square eats cheaply at lunch and well at dinner. These cover both, plus the bars that have outlasted the rent increases.

Felipe's Taqueria and its roof

21 Brattle StRed Line to Harvard, 1-minute walkDaily, late morning until 2am most nights; check before you goBurritos $10–14; counter service, full bar upstairs

The square's late-night institution: a two-story taqueria with a counter line downstairs and a rooftop deck upstairs with a bar, heaters and a view over Brattle Street to the square. The burritos are big and good, the margaritas are cheap by Cambridge standards, and the roof is the only outdoor rooftop in the square and open until late. It is where a night out in Harvard Square ends. For more see the best late-night food in Boston.

Cardullo's Gourmet Shoppe

6 Brattle StRed Line to Harvard, 1-minute walkDaily, daytime; check before you goSandwiches around $12–15

Since 1950, a small shop on the corner of Brattle packed with imported chocolate, cheese, tinned fish, British biscuits, hot sauces and a sandwich counter that makes the best lunch to take to the Yard. It is the one grocery in the square that has not been replaced by a chain and is the right place to buy a present for someone who likes food.

Alden & Harlow and Waypoint: the dinners

Alden & Harlow, 40 Brattle St (below the Brattle Theatre) · Waypoint, 1030 Massachusetts AveRed Line to Harvard; Waypoint is a 10-minute walk toward CentralDinner nightly; reservations recommendedPlates $16–40

Alden & Harlow is a basement under the Brattle Theatre with an American small-plates menu that has been the square's best dinner for a decade; the secret burger, a few a night and not on the menu, is a Cambridge legend, and the brunch is strong. Waypoint, by the same chef, is coastal Italian and seafood on the way toward Central Square, with a raw bar, pizzas and a serious cocktail list. Both are dark, loud and good.

Giulia and Russell House Tavern

Giulia, 1682 Massachusetts Ave · Russell House Tavern, 14 JFK StGiulia is a 12-minute walk north toward Porter; Russell House is in the squareDinner; Giulia books weeks outPasta $28–36 at Giulia; $18–35 at Russell House

Giulia is the pasta restaurant Cambridge people save up for, a small room with a communal pasta table at the back where the dough is rolled during the day. It is worth the walk and the wait for a reservation. Russell House Tavern, in the middle of the square, is a big two-level tavern with a long bar, oysters, a good burger and a menu that works for a group with mixed opinions.

Charlie's Kitchen, Grendel's Den and Shays: the bars

Charlie's Kitchen, 10 Eliot St · Grendel's Den, 89 Winthrop St · Shays Pub, 58 JFK StAll within 3 minutes of the stationAfternoons until latePints $7–10

Charlie's Kitchen has served a double cheeseburger and cheap beer since 1951, with a jukebox downstairs, a bar upstairs and the best beer garden in the square out back. Grendel's Den, in a basement on a side street since 1971, won a Supreme Court case to get its liquor license and has a half-price food happy hour that students have relied on for generations. Shays is a small below-grade wine and beer bar with a patio on JFK Street and a regular crowd of graduate students and locals. Any of the three is a better bar than anything on the main drag.

Coffee: Tatte, L.A. Burdick and the Coop's café

Tatte, 1288 Massachusetts Ave · L.A. Burdick, 52 Brattle St · Flour Bakery, 114 Mount Auburn StAll in the square

Tatte's Harvard Square branch is on the corner opposite the Yard and is the default study café. L.A. Burdick is a chocolate shop with a small café that makes the best hot chocolate in the region, dark, thick and served in a cup that is bigger than it needs to be; go in winter. Flour on Mount Auburn Street is the Cambridge outpost of Joanne Chang's bakery, with sticky buns and sandwiches. The full list is in the best cafés in Boston.

The bookstores of Harvard Square

There were once more than twenty. Five good ones remain, and they are the square's real signature.

StoreAddressSinceGo for
Harvard Book Store1256 Massachusetts Ave1932New books upstairs, used and remainders in the basement, author events most nights
Grolier Poetry Book Shop6 Plympton St1927Poetry only; the oldest poetry bookstore in the country
The Harvard Coop1400 Massachusetts Ave1882Textbooks, Harvard gear, a big general bookstore run by Barnes & Noble
Raven Used Books23 Church St2005 in the squareScholarly used books at low prices; the best browse in Cambridge
Schoenhof's (online only)Formerly Mount Auburn St1856Foreign-language books; the shop closed in 2017 but survives as a mail-order business

The Harvard Book Store is independent (it has never been owned by the university, despite the name) and is the anchor: a long, deep shop opposite the Yard with well-chosen new books on the ground floor, a basement of used books and publisher remainders where the bargains are, and a reading series that brings a well-known author through most weeknights, often free. The Grolier, around the corner on Plympton Street, is a single room lined to the ceiling with poetry and nothing else, founded in 1927 and visited by nearly every American poet of the last century; the staff will find you something. Raven on Church Street is where the professors sell their review copies, which means academic and literary used books at a fraction of the price, and it is the shop most likely to send you home with something you had not planned on. The Coop is the big one, and worth a walk through for the Harvard section and the building. The children's bookstore that used to anchor the corner at 1 JFK Street, the Curious George shop, closed in 2020 and is missed. Allow an hour for the circuit; it makes a good rainy afternoon.

05

Film, music and nightlife in Harvard Square

The Brattle Theatre

40 Brattle StRed Line to Harvard, 2-minute walkScreenings daily; check the calendarAround $14; check before you go

A single-screen repertory cinema in an 1890 building that has been showing films since 1953, when a pair of Harvard graduates started running Humphrey Bogart double features during exam week and accidentally invented the Bogart cult. The nonprofit that runs it programs classics, restorations, foreign film, cult series and new independent releases, with a Casablanca screening around Valentine's Day every year since the 1950s. The projection is from behind the screen, which is rare. It is the best cinema in the Boston area and the one to build an evening around.

Club Passim

47 Palmer St, in the alley between Church St and Brattle StRed Line to Harvard, 2-minute walkShows most nights; dinner served from the café menuTickets $15–40; check before you go

A 100-seat basement folk club that opened in 1958 as Club 47 and, as Passim since 1969, has hosted Joan Baez, Bob Dylan, Joni Mitchell, Suzanne Vega and nearly everyone else who has picked up an acoustic guitar since. It is a nonprofit, the room is tiny and all-ages, the food is vegetarian and decent, and the Tuesday open mic is where the next round of local singer-songwriters comes from. It is one of the last places of its kind in the country and it still feels like it.

The Sinclair

52 Church StRed Line to Harvard, 2-minute walkShows most nights; restaurant and bar open from eveningTickets $20–45

A 525-capacity music venue and restaurant that opened in 2012 and brought touring indie rock, hip-hop and electronic acts back to the square after years without a mid-size room. Standing room with a balcony, good sound, and a gastropub in front that is a reasonable dinner before the show. Check the calendar; it is the reason a lot of Bostonians cross the river on a weeknight.

The American Repertory Theater and Sanders Theatre

A.R.T. at the Loeb Drama Center, 64 Brattle St · Sanders Theatre, Memorial Hall, 45 Quincy StRed Line to Harvard, 5–7-minute walkSeasonal programs; check before you go

The A.R.T. is Harvard's professional theater company and a regular source of Broadway transfers; its Oberon second stage nearby closed in 2022, but the main house runs a full season. Sanders Theatre, inside the enormous Victorian Gothic Memorial Hall, is a 1,000-seat wooden amphitheater from 1875 with some of the best acoustics in the region, used for chamber music, folk and lectures. Memorial Hall's transept, a stained-glass memorial to Harvard's Civil War dead, is open to walk through most days and is worth five minutes even without a show.

06

History: from Newtowne to the Square

Cambridge was laid out in 1630 as Newtowne, a fortified village on the north bank of the Charles that was meant to be the capital of the Massachusetts Bay Colony. It lost that role to Boston within a few years, but in 1636 the colony's legislature voted to found a college here, and two years later a young minister named John Harvard died and left it his books and half his money, so they named it after him. The town was renamed Cambridge in 1638 after the English university most of the founders had attended. The Old Burying Ground between Christ Church and the First Parish on Garden Street, with graves from the 1630s, and the street grid of the square itself, are what survives of Newtowne.

For its first two centuries the square was a village center: the college on one side, a market and taverns on the other, and Brattle Street running west past the estates of wealthy Loyalists, which is why it was called Tory Row. When the Revolution came in 1775 the Loyalists fled, the Continental Army camped on Cambridge Common, and Washington took over the Vassall house on Brattle Street, later Longfellow's, as his headquarters. The elm on the Common under which he is said to have taken command of the army fell in 1923; a plaque marks it.

The 1800s made the square: the university grew from a few hundred students to a few thousand, horsecars and then streetcars connected it to Boston, and Cambridge became a city in 1846. The subway arrived in 1912, ending at Harvard, and the station's kiosk and the newsstand that later filled it became the square's landmark. The mid-1900s were the square's bohemian peak, with Club 47 and the folk revival, the Brattle's Bogart festivals, dozens of bookstores, the Harvard Lampoon in its mock-castle on Bow Street, and a counterculture that made the Pit a gathering place for forty years.

Since the 1990s the story has been rent. Chains replaced the record shops and most of the bookstores, the Tasty diner and the Wursthaus closed, the newsstand kiosk went dark in 2019 (it has since reopened as a visitor and community space), and the Pit was filled in 2022. What has held is what the university and the nonprofits own or protect: the museums, the Brattle, Passim, the Yard, the Longfellow House, and the bookstores that were stubborn enough to stay. The square in 2026 is more polished and less strange than it was, and still, by any reasonable standard, one of the best small urban centers in the country.

07

Side streets and beyond the square

The triangle at the station is small. The good walking is in every direction from it.

Brattle Street and Tory Row

West from the square, Brattle Street becomes one of the finest residential streets in the country: a mile of 1700s and 1800s mansions under old trees, seven of them Loyalist estates from before the Revolution. The Longfellow House is the one you can enter; the Hooper-Lee-Nichols House at 159, run by History Cambridge, is open some days; the rest are private. The walk out to Mount Auburn Cemetery follows it the whole way.

Cambridge Common and Radcliffe Yard

North of the square across Mass Ave, the Common is where the Continental Army camped, with a Civil War monument, a playground and a set of cannon. Behind it, Radcliffe Yard off Garden Street is a small, quiet quadrangle of brick around a lawn, the old heart of the women's college that merged into Harvard, and one of the loveliest spots on the campus. Christ Church on Garden Street, from 1761, is the oldest church building in Cambridge and has a musket-ball hole from the Revolution by the door.

Plympton Street, Bow Street and the Lampoon

South of Mass Ave, past the Harvard Book Store, is the quarter where the Grolier, the Harvard Lampoon's 1909 mock-Flemish castle at 44 Bow Street, the Harvard Advocate and a few river houses sit on short, quiet streets. The Lampoon building, with a face made from its windows and door, is the oddest building in Cambridge and cannot be entered, but the corner of Bow and Plympton is worth the detour.

Winthrop Street and Eliot Street

The small block between JFK Street and the Charles Hotel, where Grendel's Den, Charlie's Kitchen and Pinocchio's Pizza (Sicilian slices since 1966, and a Facebook founding-story location) sit on streets that have not changed much in fifty years. Winthrop Park is a pocket park with benches for eating a slice.

Toward Porter and Central

Mass Ave runs north to Porter Square (Giulia, a Japanese food hall in the Porter Exchange, the Red Line one stop on) and southeast to Central Square (the bars, Toscanini's ice cream and the Middle East club), each about a 20-minute walk or one Red Line stop. The Kendall and Central guide covers the other direction.

08

Harvard Square events through the year

The square runs on the university calendar. Commencement in late May fills the Yard with tents and closes parts of it for a week, and the days before it are the busiest of the year; move-in in late August and early September is nearly as crowded. The Head of the Charles, on the third weekend of October, is the largest two-day regatta in the world, and the river banks from the Weeks Bridge to the Eliot Bridge are lined with tens of thousands of spectators; the square is packed all weekend. Every other November, the Harvard–Yale game at Harvard Stadium across the river brings alumni and tailgates.

The Harvard Square Business Association runs the civic events: MayFair on the first Sunday in May, with stages and food stalls filling the closed streets; Oktoberfest on a Sunday in October, which coincides with the HONK! festival of activist street bands parading down Mass Ave from Davis Square; a holiday shopping weekend with carolers and a tree in early December; and outdoor music through the summer. The Brattle has annual series (the Casablanca screening in February, a summer of Hitchcock or noir), Passim hosts the Boston Celtic Music Festival in January and a campfire festival on holiday weekends, and the museums run free lectures most weeks in term. 09

Tips for a first visit to Harvard Square

Check the museum days. The Art Museums are closed Mondays; the Natural History Museum is open daily. Plan the day around that and you can do both, with the Yard in between, in about four hours.

Book the tour if you want it, skip it if you don't. The free campus tours are good and fill up in fall and spring. But the Yard is open, and a slow walk through it with the plaques takes twenty minutes and is enough for many people.

Bring cash for Bartley's. And go at 2pm rather than noon on a weekday, or expect to wait for a table on the sidewalk. It is closed Sundays.

Come on a Sunday for the river. Memorial Drive is closed to cars from late April to mid-November, and the walk from JFK Park to the Weeks Bridge and beyond is as good as the city gets.

Walk to Mount Auburn one way and take the bus the other. The 71 and 73 run every few minutes from the busway under the station. Thirty minutes there on foot along Brattle Street, ten minutes back on the bus.

Stay for the evening. The square is better after 6pm than at 2pm: the tour groups leave, the students come out, and the Brattle, Passim and the Sinclair are all within a two-minute walk of each other.

Wear layers by the river. The Charles has its own wind, and the Weeks Bridge at sunset in October is beautiful and cold.

Pair it with the rest of Cambridge. Kendall Square and MIT are two stops back toward Boston; Davis Square is two stops out. Our weekend itinerary fits Harvard Square into a Boston trip, and the Boston neighborhoods hub has the rest.

FAQ

Harvard Square: common questions

What is Harvard Square known for?

Harvard Square is the commercial center of Cambridge, wrapped around Harvard University's original campus. It is known for Harvard Yard, the Harvard Art Museums and the Glass Flowers at the natural history museum, an unusual number of independent bookstores, the Brattle Theatre, the folk club Passim, street performers, late-night burritos, and being ten minutes from Boston on the Red Line.

How do you get to Harvard Square from Boston?

Take the Red Line outbound (toward Alewife) to Harvard station. It is about 10 minutes from Park Street and 7 from Charles/MGH. The station exits directly into the square. Buses from Allston, Watertown and Belmont use the underground busway at the station. Walking from Kendall Square along the river takes about 40 minutes.

Can you visit Harvard Yard without a tour?

Yes. Harvard Yard is open to the public and you can walk through the gates any time. The buildings, including Widener Library, are not open to visitors. Free student-led tours run most days from the Smith Campus Center at 1350 Massachusetts Avenue; check the schedule and book ahead in busy months.

Are the Harvard museums free?

The Harvard Art Museums at 32 Quincy Street have been free to everyone since 2023 and are closed Mondays. The Harvard Museum of Natural History, with the Glass Flowers, charges around $15 for adults, and the ticket also covers the Peabody Museum next door; Massachusetts residents get in free on Sunday mornings. Check current hours and prices before you go.

How long should you spend in Harvard Square?

Half a day covers the Yard, one museum, a bookstore and lunch. A full day adds the second museum, a walk to the river or Mount Auburn Cemetery, and a show at the Brattle, the Sinclair or Passim in the evening. It is the easiest half-day trip from Boston on the T.

Is Harvard Square worth visiting at night?

Yes. The Brattle Theatre shows repertory film, Club Passim has folk music most nights, the Sinclair books touring bands, Felipe's rooftop is open late, Charlie's Kitchen and Grendel's Den are good bars, and the Yard is quiet and lit. It is calmer than Boston's nightlife districts and safe to walk.
