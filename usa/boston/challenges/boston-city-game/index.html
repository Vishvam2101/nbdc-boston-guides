A city exploration game is the thing you invent for yourself when you have lived somewhere long enough to stop looking at it. Somebody asks whether you have noticed the purple windows on Beacon Street, you have not, and the next Saturday you go looking for them and notice four other things on the way. The four routes on this page are that idea written down: specific things to find in one Boston neighborhood, in a walking order that makes sense, with the address and nearest T stop for each.

Boston suits a city game because it is small and detailed. Each route is 1.5 to 2.5 miles, has seven stops, and takes 90 minutes to two and a half hours. Some stops are landmarks you would visit anyway. Most are the details around them: a weathervane, a footbridge, a statue's shoe, a plaque set into the sidewalk. Every stop is real and free to see from outside.

You can follow the routes from this page. In the no bad days club app they are challenges: the same stops on a map with a checklist you tick as you reach each one. No timer, no host, nothing to buy.

01

What a city exploration game is, and why Boston suits it

A city game sits between a walking tour and a scavenger hunt. A tour tells you about a place while you stand in front of it. A scavenger hunt gives you clues and a clock. A city exploration game gives you a list of specific things to find in a specific area and lets you get on with it. Finding the grasshopper on Faneuil Hall, working out which Duckling is Ouack, spotting the Skinny House from the burying ground: small satisfactions, and a route of seven adds up to an afternoon that feels earned.

Boston is good for this for three reasons. It is walkable: the four neighborhoods here are each a mile or two across, and three of them touch. It is layered: a 1660 burying ground shares a block with a 1970s office tower. And it is legible: the neighborhoods have clear edges, so a Beacon Hill route feels nothing like a Seaport route.

The routes are for visitors with a day or two who want structure without a group tour, and for locals who want a reason to walk a neighborhood they have taken for granted. The Boston challenges hub explains the format and links to the other routes, including the full Freedom Trail challenge.

02

How a Boston exploration game route is structured

Each route starts and ends at a T stop, stays inside one neighborhood or two that share a border, and has seven stops in an order that does not double back. Stops are one of three types. Landmarks are the anchors: the State House, Trinity Church, the ICA, Harvard Yard. Photo stops are places where the task is a specific shot: Acorn Street looking downhill, the skyline from Fan Pier. Detail stops are the things most people walk past: the Massacre ring, the purple panes, the plaque under your feet. The detail stops are the ones people remember.

A stop counts as found when you are physically there and have seen the thing. In the app, that means opening the challenge, finding the stop on the map and marking it done. On paper, a tick and, by convention, a photo. The route is complete when every stop is ticked.

One rule that helps: do the stops in order the first time. The order is set so the walk flows and the best view comes last. Second time through, do what you like.

03

Route 1: Downtown and the Freedom Trail core

Start at Park Street. About 1.5 miles, 90 minutes to two hours. The Freedom Trail's densest stretch as a detail hunt. For all 16 stops, use the Freedom Trail challenge.

Brewer Fountain and the start of the red line

Boston Common, near Park St and Tremont StPark Street (Red/Green)Free

The 1868 bronze fountain is right outside the station. Find where the red brick line begins by the Visitor Information Center at 139 Tremont Street; you follow it for the next hour.

The gold dome of the State House

24 Beacon StPark Street (Red/Green)Free tours weekdays; check before you go

Wood, then copper from Paul Revere's company, gilded since 1874. Photo stop: the dome framed by the Common's trees. Across Beacon Street, find the Shaw Memorial relief honouring the 54th Massachusetts.

Granary Burying Ground

Tremont St, next to Park Street ChurchPark Street (Red/Green)Free, closes at dusk

Paul Revere, Samuel Adams, John Hancock and the Massacre victims. Detail: the stone of Mary Goose, sometimes claimed as Mother Goose. Doubtful claim, real stone.

The Franklin statue and the school mosaic

45 School St, Old City Hall courtyardPark Street or State (Blue/Orange)Free, sidewalk

Franklin's statue marks the site of Boston Latin, the country's first public school, from 1635. The stop is the mosaic in the sidewalk that most people step over.

Old State House and the Massacre ring

206 Washington StState (Blue/Orange)Exterior free; admission inside

The 1713 building with the lion and unicorn on the gable, where the Declaration was read from the balcony in 1776. Below it, in the traffic island at State and Congress, a ring of cobblestones marks the Boston Massacre. Stand on it.

The grasshopper on Faneuil Hall

1 Faneuil Hall SquareGovernment Center (Blue/Green)Great Hall free

The gilded copper weathervane has been up since 1742. Look up from the Dock Square side, then go upstairs to the Great Hall, where park rangers give free talks. Quincy Market next door is the food stop.

Long Wharf and the harbor

End of State St at the waterAquarium (Blue)Free

Walk down State Street under the Custom House tower to the end of the wharf, where the ferries leave and the harbor opens up. Finish here, or continue straight into Route 3, which starts from the same spot.

04

Route 2: Back Bay and Beacon Hill

Start at Charles/MGH. About 2.5 miles, two to two and a half hours. The prettiest route and the best for photos; weekday evenings have the fewest people on Acorn Street. See our Beacon Hill and Back Bay guides.

Acorn Street

Acorn St, between West Cedar St and Willow StCharles/MGH (Red)Public street, free

Walk up Charles Street past the gas lamps, which are still gas, then up Chestnut and left onto West Cedar to the cobblestone lane on every most-photographed-street list. The shot is from the bottom looking up.

Louisburg Square

Between Mt Vernon St and Pinckney StCharles/MGH (Red)Private park, public streets

A private oval of green ringed by the most expensive houses in the city. Louisa May Alcott lived at number 10. The stop is the iron railing and the bow-fronted houses on the west side.

The purple windows on Beacon Street

63 and 64 Beacon St, facing the CommonPark Street or Arlington (Green)Exterior, free

A few panes turned lavender in sunlight because of a flaw in a batch of English glass from the 1820s, and became a status symbol. Find them from the sidewalk with the sun behind you.

The Ducklings in the Public Garden

Near the Beacon St and Charles St cornerArlington (Green)Free

Mrs Mallard and eight bronze ducklings from the 1941 book. Detail stop: name all eight (Jack, Kack, Lack, Mack, Nack, Ouack, Pack, Quack). Then cross the lagoon on the tiny suspension bridge.

Commonwealth Avenue Mall

Comm Ave from Arlington St to Dartmouth StArlington (Green)Free

Leave the Garden by the Washington statue and walk the tree-lined mall. The stop is the Boston Women's Memorial between Fairfield and Gloucester: three bronze figures at street level, off their pedestals.

Trinity Church reflected in the Hancock tower

Copley Square, 206 Clarendon StCopley (Green)Exterior free; admission to tour inside

Turn down Dartmouth to Copley Square. Photo stop: the 1877 church reflected in the glass of 200 Clarendon. Stand on the Boylston Street side of the square for the cleanest reflection.

Bates Hall at the Boston Public Library

700 Boylston StCopley (Green)Mon–Thu 9–8, Fri–Sat 9–5, Sun 11–5, free

In through the Dartmouth Street doors and up the marble stairs to the reading room: green lamps, a 50-foot barrel vault, silence. Sit for five minutes, find the courtyard, then finish along Newbury Street to Hynes station.

05

Route 3: Seaport and the Harborwalk

Start at Aquarium. About 2.5 miles, two hours. Flat and easy, with the skyline across the water the whole way. Best in the two hours before sunset. More in our Seaport guide.

Long Wharf and the Custom House tower

Long Wharf, end of State StAquarium (Blue)Free

Start at the end of the wharf and turn around: the 1915 Custom House tower, Boston's first skyscraper, is straight up State Street. The stop is the clock face.

Christopher Columbus Park trellis

110 Atlantic AveAquarium (Blue)Free

Walk north to the waterfront park at the edge of the North End. The 260-foot wisteria trellis is the stop, lit blue in winter. Look up the harbor for the Zakim Bridge.

Rowes Wharf arch

Atlantic Ave at Rowes WharfAquarium (Blue)Public walkway, free

Back south along the Harborwalk past the Aquarium to the huge arch of the Boston Harbor Hotel. Photo stop: the arch framing the harbor from the Atlantic Avenue side.

The Tea Party ships from the Congress Street bridge

Congress St bridge over Fort Point ChannelSouth Station (Red), 5-minute walkBridge free; museum charges admission

Follow Fort Point Channel to the bridge. The replica tea ships are moored below and you do not need a ticket to see them. The giant milk bottle opposite belongs to the Children's Museum.

Fan Pier Park

Fan Pier, in front of the Moakley CourthouseCourthouse (Silver Line)Free

Back north to Seaport Boulevard, then around the water side of the courthouse to the small park at the tip. The best close-up skyline view in the city, especially after dark. Everyone takes the same picture and it is still worth taking.

The ICA and its harbor steps

25 Harbor Shore DrCourthouse (Silver Line)Steps free; galleries free Thu 5–9pm

A glass box cantilevered over the harbor. The outdoor grandstand steps facing the water are open without a ticket and are where to sit at the end of the walk.

Seaport Common and Harbor Way

85 Northern AveCourthouse or World Trade Center (Silver Line)Free

Finish on the small lawn and the pedestrian Harbor Way running inland from it, which usually has a public art installation or a seasonal market. The Silver Line runs back to South Station.

06

Route 4: Cambridge and Harvard Square

Start at Harvard. About 2 miles, two hours. Campus, bookstores and the river. Best after 5pm when the square is busiest, or Sunday morning when the Yard is quiet. More in our Harvard Square guide.

The John Harvard statue and its three lies

Harvard Yard, in front of University HallHarvard (Red)Yard open to the public, free

The plaque says "John Harvard, Founder, 1638." He was not the founder, the college dates from 1636, and the face is a student's. The stop is the shiny left shoe, rubbed for luck.

Widener Library steps

Harvard Yard, south sideHarvard (Red)Steps public; interior Harvard ID only

Built in memory of Harry Elkins Widener, who died on the Titanic. You cannot go in, but the steps are the best seat in the Yard and the photo stop is Memorial Church across the lawn.

Harvard Art Museums

32 Quincy StHarvard (Red)Tue–Sun 10–5, free

Out the east side of the Yard to the museum with the Renzo Piano glass roof. Free to everyone. The stop is the central courtyard under the roof.

Harvard Book Store basement

1256 Massachusetts AveHarvard (Red)Open late most nights; check before you go

The independent bookstore on the square since 1932. The stop is the used-book basement. Buy something small; this is the kind of shop the game is meant to send you into.

Grolier Poetry Book Shop

6 Plympton StHarvard (Red)Afternoon hours; check before you go

A one-room poetry bookshop off Mass Ave, open since 1927 and reputedly the oldest of its kind in the country. Small enough to miss, which is why it is a stop.

Weeks Footbridge

Charles River, foot of DeWolfe StHarvard (Red), 10-minute walkFree

Down DeWolfe Street to the river and onto the arched brick footbridge. The stop is the middle, with the Harvard houses on one bank and the Business School on the other. During the Head of the Charles in October, this is the best spot on the course.

The Lampoon castle

44 Bow St, corner of Mt Auburn StHarvard (Red)Exterior, free

Return along Mount Auburn Street to the Harvard Lampoon building, a small castle whose front looks like a face. Find the face; that is the last stop. Harvard station is three minutes away.

08

Playing the Boston city game with friends

Three to six people is the sweet spot. Larger than that and it becomes two groups walking at different speeds, and the slow one always has the person with the map.

Give people jobs. One navigator, one reader who says what the stop is and what to look for, one photographer whose pictures are the record. Rotate at the food stop. It sounds fussy and it is the single thing that keeps a group moving.

Race it, or don't. Two teams starting the same route from opposite ends is a good birthday format, meeting in the middle: Quincy Market on Route 1, the Public Garden on Route 2, the Congress Street bridge on Route 3. First team to arrive with every stop photographed wins; the loser buys the round.

Plan the end. Every route finishes somewhere you can sit. Decide in advance where dinner or drinks are so the game ends on purpose. Our things to do with friends guide has more group ideas, and happy hours covers the after.

Keep the app on one phone. If you are ticking stops off in the app, nominate one person so the group has a single record. Everyone else takes pictures.

09

Playing solo

Alone is arguably the better way to play, and certainly the faster one. A route gives a solo walk a purpose, which is what solo travel and a first week in a new city both lack. You are not wandering; you are looking for the purple windows, and the wandering happens on the way.

Solo players finish in about 70 percent of the group time. The trade-off is food stops, so pick counters: the Quincy Market halls on Route 1, Trident on Newbury for Route 2, the Seaport food trucks on Route 3, any Harvard Square counter on Route 4. Our things to do alone in Boston guide lists places that are comfortable on your own.

If you are new to the city, do one route a week for four weeks. By the end you will have walked the four central neighborhoods with intent. The new to Boston guide covers the rest of settling in, and how to make friends in Boston covers the part the routes cannot.

Safety note: all four routes are in busy, well-lit central areas and are fine alone at any reasonable hour. The Harborwalk is the quietest after dark on winter weeknights.

FAQ

Boston city game: common questions

What is a Boston city exploration game?

A Boston city exploration game is a self-guided walking route through one neighborhood with a list of specific stops to find: landmarks, hidden details and photo spots. You walk at your own pace and tick off each stop when you reach it. The four routes on this page are available as challenges in the no bad days club app, where each stop is a checklist item on a map.

How long does each route take?

Between 90 minutes and two and a half hours, depending on the route and how many photos you take. Downtown is the shortest at about 1.5 miles; Back Bay and Beacon Hill is the longest at about 2.5 miles. Add 30 to 45 minutes for a food stop.

Can you play the Boston city game alone?

Yes, and many people do. The routes work as a structured solo walk with a purpose, which is useful if you are new to the city or travelling alone. Solo players finish faster and take better photos because nobody is waiting.

Does the game cost anything?

No. The routes are free to follow from this page or in the app. The only costs are transit, food, and any stop you choose to go inside that charges admission. Every stop on these four routes is free to see from outside, and most are free to enter.

Which route should you do first?

Downtown if it is your first time in Boston, because it covers the Freedom Trail landmarks. Back Bay and Beacon Hill for the prettiest streets and best photos. Seaport for an easy flat walk with water views, ideally at dusk. Cambridge if you have done the Boston side or are staying near Harvard.

Is the Boston city game suitable for kids?

The Downtown and Back Bay routes are best for children: flat, stroller-friendly, with things to spot such as the Ducklings statues, the gold State House dome and the grasshopper weathervane on Faneuil Hall. Keep it to one route per day.
