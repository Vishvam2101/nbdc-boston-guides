By no bad days club, BostonUpdated September 8, 202612 min read

Most people who walk the Freedom Trail do about half of it. They start on the Common, follow the red line through Downtown, reach Faneuil Hall, eat something, wander into the North End, and stop. The last mile over the bridge to Charlestown, with the ship and the monument at the end, gets skipped more often than not. That is a shame, because the ship is free, the monument is free, and the ferry back across the harbor is the best ten minutes of the whole day.

The Freedom Trail challenge is a way of not skipping it. The rule is simple: all 16 official stops, in order, each one ticked off when you are standing in front of it. This page lists every stop with its address, the thing to look for when you get there, and whether going inside is free or costs money. It also gives you three timings, a fast walk, a normal day and a thorough one, so you can decide in advance what kind of day it is.

You can do the challenge from this page. In the no bad days club app, the trail is available as a challenge with the same 16 stops on a map and a checklist you tick as you reach each one. For the history, the tours and the practical background, our general Freedom Trail guide is the companion to this page; this one is the checklist.

01

How the Freedom Trail challenge works

The Freedom Trail is a 2.5-mile route marked by a red line, mostly brick and sometimes paint, that runs from Boston Common through Downtown and the North End to Charlestown. It was laid out in 1951 and connects 16 sites from the colonial and Revolutionary period. Walking it is free and needs no ticket, no guide and no booking. The line does the navigating.

The challenge is the trail with a finish line. You do the 16 stops in the official order, and each one counts as done when you have reached it and found the thing this page tells you to look for. On paper that means a tick and, by convention, a photo. In the app, the trail is a challenge: the stops are on a map, you mark each one done when you are there, and the challenge completes when the sixteenth is ticked. There is no timer unless you set one yourself.

Two things make the challenge different from just walking the trail. First, the order and the finish: you commit to Charlestown at the start, which changes how you pace the day. Second, the detail at each stop: rather than reading a plaque and moving on, you are looking for something specific, the Mother Goose stone, the lion and unicorn, the grasshopper, the Skinny House, the copper on the ship's hull. It makes the trail slower in the right places and faster in the others. For the format in general, and the other Boston routes, see the Boston challenges hub.

Start early. Between 8am and 10am the Downtown stretch is nearly empty, the burying grounds are open, and you reach the North End as the bakeries open. Sites that charge admission mostly open at 9 or 10, so a fast walker who leaves at 8 hits them as the doors open.

02

Stops 1–11: Boston Common to Faneuil Hall

About a mile, and the densest stretch of the trail. Eleven stops in under an hour if you keep moving, two hours if you go inside the paid sites. Start at Park Street station.

Boston Common

Visitor Information Center, 139 Tremont StPark Street (Red/Green)Always openFree

The oldest public park in the country, bought by the town in 1634 as common grazing land. British troops camped here before Lexington and Concord. The trail begins at the Visitor Information Center on Tremont Street, where the red line starts; pick it up there so the count is honest. Look for the Brewer Fountain near Park Street station, and, up the slope toward Beacon Street, the Soldiers and Sailors Monument on the hill.

Look for: the start of the red line in the sidewalk outside the Visitor Center.

Massachusetts State House

24 Beacon StPark Street (Red/Green)Mon–Fri, roughly 9–5; closed weekendsFree; free tours on weekdays, check before you go

Charles Bulfinch's 1798 capitol on the crest of Beacon Hill, on land that was John Hancock's cow pasture. The dome was wooden, then copper-clad by Paul Revere's company in 1802, then gilded in 1874. Inside, the Hall of Flags and the Sacred Cod, a carved wooden fish that hangs in the House chamber, are the things to see if you go in. Across Beacon Street, at the top of the Common, is the Shaw Memorial by Augustus Saint-Gaudens, honouring Robert Gould Shaw and the 54th Massachusetts, the first Black regiment raised in the North.

Look for: the gold dome from the Common path below, and the Shaw Memorial relief opposite.

Park Street Church

1 Park St, corner of Tremont StPark Street (Red/Green)Open for visitors in summer; check before you goFree

The 217-foot steeple was the first thing travellers saw coming into Boston in 1809, and the corner is still called Brimstone Corner, either for the fiery preaching or for the gunpowder stored in the crypt during the War of 1812. William Lloyd Garrison gave his first major anti-slavery speech here in 1829, and "America" ("My Country, 'Tis of Thee") was first sung on the steps in 1831.

Look for: the steeple from the middle of the Common, then the plaque on the Tremont Street side.

Granary Burying Ground

Tremont St, next to Park Street ChurchPark Street (Red/Green)Daily, roughly 9–5; later in summerFree

Boston's third-oldest cemetery, from 1660, named for the town grain store that once stood where the church is now. Paul Revere, Samuel Adams, John Hancock, James Otis, Robert Treat Paine, the five victims of the Boston Massacre and Benjamin Franklin's parents are all here. The Franklin obelisk is in the centre. Revere's grave has two markers, a small original and a larger later one, on the far side from the gate.

Look for: the stone of Mary Goose, sometimes claimed as Mother Goose, near the Tremont Street wall. The claim is doubtful; the stone is real.

King's Chapel and Burying Ground

58 Tremont St, corner of School StPark Street or Government CenterChapel hours vary by season; burying ground daily until duskBurying ground free; chapel suggested donation, check before you go

The burying ground is the oldest in the city, from 1630, and the chapel next to it was the first Anglican church in New England, built in 1754 of Quincy granite around an earlier wooden one. The chapel's bell was recast by Paul Revere in 1816 and is still rung. Inside are box pews and a pulpit from 1717, the oldest still in use in the country. The burying ground holds John Winthrop, the first governor, and Mary Chilton, said to be the first woman off the Mayflower.

Look for: the winged skulls on the older gravestones, and the plain granite exterior with no steeple, because the money ran out.

Boston Latin School site and Benjamin Franklin statue

45 School St, courtyard of Old City HallPark Street or State (Blue/Orange)Always accessibleFree

School Street is named for the school that stood here from 1635, the first public school in the country, and the one Franklin, Hancock and Samuel Adams attended. Franklin dropped out. His statue, from 1856, stands in the courtyard of Old City Hall, the 1865 French Second Empire building that is now offices and a steakhouse. The stop itself is on the ground: a mosaic in the sidewalk marking the school's original location.

Look for: the mosaic in the pavement in front of the courtyard, which most people step over.

Old Corner Bookstore

283 Washington St, corner of School StState (Blue/Orange)Ground floor is a restaurantFree, exterior only

The oldest commercial building in the city, from 1718, built as an apothecary and house. From 1832 it was the bookshop and publishing house of Ticknor and Fields, which published Hawthorne, Emerson, Thoreau, Longfellow, Stowe and the Atlantic Monthly. It is now a fast-casual restaurant, which is not what anyone wants, but the brick and the gambrel roof are the original ones and the building survived because the trail exists.

Look for: the plaque on the School Street side listing the authors published here.

Old South Meeting House

310 Washington StState or Downtown CrossingDaily, roughly 10–5; check before you goAdmission charged; combined ticket with Old State House

The largest building in colonial Boston, and the room where five thousand people met on 16 December 1773 before the Tea Party. Samuel Adams gave the signal from the pulpit. The British used it as a riding school during the siege, which is why the interior is a 19th-century restoration. It is run by Revolutionary Spaces along with the Old State House; one ticket covers both, and if you are going inside one paid site on the Downtown stretch, this pair is the one.

Look for: the clock on the tower, in place since 1770 and still running.

Old State House

206 Washington StState (Blue/Orange), directly belowDaily, roughly 10–5; check before you goAdmission charged; combined ticket with Old South

The 1713 seat of colonial government, now sitting in a canyon of towers at the head of State Street with a subway station underneath it. The Declaration of Independence was read to Boston from the balcony on 18 July 1776, and the lion and unicorn on the gable, symbols of the crown, were pulled down that day and burned. The ones up there now are replicas, put back in 1882. The balcony reading is re-enacted every Fourth of July.

Look for: the lion and the unicorn on the State Street gable.

Boston Massacre site

Traffic island at State St and Congress St, below the Old State House balconyState (Blue/Orange)Always accessibleFree

On 5 March 1770, British soldiers fired into a crowd here and killed five men, including Crispus Attucks. The site is marked by a ring of cobblestones set into the traffic island, with a plaque, and it is easy to miss because it is under your feet in the middle of a crossing. John Adams defended the soldiers at trial and got most of them acquitted, which he later called one of the best things he ever did.

Look for: the cobblestone ring. Stand on it.

Faneuil Hall

1 Faneuil Hall Square, Dock Square sideGovernment Center (Blue/Green) or HaymarketGreat Hall daily, roughly 9–5; check before you goFree; National Park Service

A market on the ground floor and a meeting hall above, given to the town by Peter Faneuil in 1742 and enlarged by Bulfinch in 1806. The Great Hall upstairs is where the town meetings that led to the Revolution were held, and where Frederick Douglass and Susan B. Anthony later spoke. Rangers give short talks there, free. The grasshopper weathervane on the cupola has been up since 1742 and was supposedly used to identify spies in 1812: if you did not know what was on top of Faneuil Hall, you were not from Boston.

Look for: the gilded grasshopper, from the Dock Square side. Then go upstairs. This is the halfway point of the challenge and the natural place to eat; see where to eat.

03

Stops 12–14: the North End

Cross under the Greenway from Faneuil Hall and follow the line up Hanover Street into the oldest neighborhood in the city. Three stops in half a mile, with the best food on the trail between them. Our North End guide covers the neighborhood properly.

Paul Revere House

19 North SquareHaymarket (Orange/Green)Daily in season, roughly 10–5; closed Mondays in winter; check before you goSmall admission charged

The oldest house in Downtown Boston, built around 1680 and bought by Revere in 1770. He lived here with his family for about thirty years, and it was from here that he set out on 18 April 1775. The house was a tenement and a cigar factory before his great-grandson saved it in 1902. It is small, the visit takes twenty minutes, and it is the one paid stop in the North End worth paying for. North Square outside it is a cobbled triangle that looks the way the whole neighborhood used to.

Look for: the overhanging second storey and the small diamond-paned casement windows, which are 17th-century, not colonial.

Old North Church

193 Salem StHaymarket or North StationDaily, roughly 10–5, later in summer; check before you goAdmission charged for the church; the courtyards outside are free

Christ Church, from 1723, the oldest church building in the city, and the steeple where two lanterns were hung on the night of 18 April 1775 to signal that the British were leaving by water. The steeple has blown down twice and is a replica; the box pews, the brass chandeliers and the bust of George Washington inside are original. Walk to it from Revere's house through the Paul Revere Mall, the brick plaza off Hanover Street that locals call the Prado, with the equestrian statue of Revere framed against the steeple.

Look for: the steeple framed by the Prado, which is the best photograph on the trail. Then the plaque inside the Prado's wall listing the North End's notable residents.

Copp's Hill Burying Ground

Hull St at Snow Hill StNorth Station (Orange/Green)Daily until duskFree

The second-oldest burying ground in the city, from 1659, on the hill above the church. Cotton and Increase Mather are here, and Prince Hall, the founder of Black Freemasonry, and about a thousand free Black Bostonians from the "New Guinea" community that lived on the hill's slope. The British fired on Charlestown from here during the Battle of Bunker Hill. From the Hull Street gate you can see the Zakim Bridge and across the water to the ship and the monument, the two stops still to go.

Look for: the Skinny House at 44 Hull Street, directly across from the gate, about ten feet wide and said to be a spite house built to block a brother's view.

04

Stops 15–16: Charlestown

The mile most people skip. From Copp's Hill, follow the line down to Commercial Street, over the Charlestown Bridge across the mouth of the Charles, and along to the Navy Yard. Both stops are free, and the way home is a ferry.

USS Constitution

Charlestown Navy Yard, Building 5, 1 Constitution RdNorth Station (Orange/Green), 15-minute walk; or the ferry to Long Wharf afterTue–Sun in season, roughly 10–4; closed Mondays; check before you goFree; photo ID required for visitors 18 and over

Launched in 1797 and still a commissioned US Navy ship, the oldest afloat in the world. She earned the name Old Ironsides in 1812 when British cannonballs bounced off her live-oak hull. Active-duty sailors give the tours and you can go below decks. Security screening at the gate is the reason for the ID. The USS Constitution Museum in the building opposite is separate, donation-suggested and good for kids. The Navy Yard itself, a working yard from 1800 to 1974, is a National Park site and free to walk.

Look for: the copper sheathing on the hull below the waterline, originally supplied by Paul Revere's mill. The trail keeps coming back to him.

Bunker Hill Monument

Monument Square, CharlestownCommunity College (Orange), 10-minute walkGrounds daily; monument climb seasonal hours, check before you goFree; climb may need a timed pass in busy seasons

A 221-foot granite obelisk on Breed's Hill, where the battle of 17 June 1775 was actually fought; Bunker Hill proper is a few blocks north. The colonists lost the ground and the British lost more than a thousand men taking it. The monument was finished in 1843 after twenty years of fundraising. The climb is 294 steps in a spiral with no elevator and a view over the harbor, the Zakim Bridge and Downtown at the top. The museum across the square is free. Walk up from the Navy Yard through Charlestown's gas-lit streets rather than along the main road; it is the prettiest half mile of the day.

Look for: the end of the red line at the base of the monument. That is stop 16. Tick it, then sit down.

Order matters here. The official order is Constitution then monument, but if the ship is closed (Mondays, and some winter days) do the monument first and check the ship's hours before you commit to the walk over the bridge. Either way, finish at the Navy Yard pier for the ferry.

Timing: fast, normal or thorough

Three ways to do the challenge, depending on how much of the day you have. All three cover the full 16 stops; they differ in how many you go inside and how long you sit down.

Whichever you pick, the pacing is front-loaded: eleven stops in the first mile, three in the next half mile, two in the last mile. Do not spend the whole morning on the Common side and arrive in the North End at 2pm with the ship closing at 4. A good rule for the normal day is to be at Faneuil Hall by 11, in the North End by 12:30, and over the bridge by 2. 07

Where to eat along the way

The trail passes through the best cheap food in the city. These are the stops that fit the route without a detour, in trail order. Hours change; check before you go.

Thinking Cup, before you start

165 Tremont StBoylston or Park Street (Green)Early to evening

A serious coffee shop on the Common's edge, two minutes from the start of the line. Get a coffee and a pastry here at 8am and take it to the Brewer Fountain. This is the only coffee stop until the North End.

Boston Public Market and Haymarket

100 Hanover St, corner of Congress StHaymarket (Orange/Green)Market most days from morning; Haymarket stalls Fri–Sat

An indoor market of Massachusetts producers, right where the trail crosses from Faneuil Hall to the North End: oysters, cider doughnuts, sandwiches, a good cheese counter. On Fridays and Saturdays the open-air Haymarket produce stalls run along Blackstone Street outside, the cheapest fruit in the city and a scene worth walking through.

Quincy Market, if you want fast

4 S Market St, behind Faneuil HallGovernment Center (Blue/Green)Daily, roughly 10–9

The food hall behind stop 11. Crowded and touristy, but a cup of chowder from one of the stalls eaten on the steps is a fair halfway lunch, and it takes fifteen minutes. The North End is better if you have 45.

Galleria Umberto

289 Hanover StHaymarket (Orange/Green)Mon–Sat lunch only, closes when the food runs out, usually by 2pm

Sicilian-style square pizza by the slice, arancini and panzarotti, cash only, standing room and a line out the door. It is the best lunch on the trail and the cheapest, and it has been on Hanover Street since 1974. If you reach it after 2pm, it is closed. Plan for it.

Mike's, Modern or Bova's for the cannoli

Mike's Pastry 300 Hanover St · Modern Pastry 257 Hanover St · Bova's Bakery 134 Salem StHaymarket (Orange/Green)Mike's and Modern daytime into evening; Bova's open 24 hours

The trail passes all three. Mike's is the famous one with the line and the string-tied boxes. Modern, across the street, fills its shells to order and is the local pick. Bova's, on the Old North Church side on Salem Street, is open around the clock and is where the neighborhood goes. Our position is Modern, but the correct approach to the challenge is to try two and decide. More in the best cannoli in Boston.

Regina Pizzeria, for a proper sit-down

11½ Thacher StHaymarket or North StationDaily, lunch and dinner

The original Regina's, from 1926, on a corner between Copp's Hill and the bridge to Charlestown. Thin, blistered, brick-oven pizza in a room that has not changed much. Slightly off the line but on your way, and the right choice for a thorough day. It is also covered in the best pizza in Boston.

Warren Tavern, at the finish

2 Pleasant St, CharlestownCommunity College (Orange)Daily, lunch until late

Built in 1780, one of the oldest taverns in Massachusetts, named for Joseph Warren who died at Bunker Hill, and visited by Paul Revere and George Washington. It is three minutes from the monument. Sit at the bar with a beer and a burger with all sixteen stops ticked. This is the correct end to the challenge.

08

Getting there, getting back and what to bring

Start at Park Street station on the Red and Green Lines; the Visitor Information Center and the start of the red line are directly outside. If you are coming from Back Bay, Arlington or Boylston on the Green Line are one stop earlier and a five-minute walk across the Common. There is no useful parking Downtown, so take the T.

From the finish, the MBTA ferry from the Charlestown Navy Yard pier, a few minutes from the ship, runs to Long Wharf every half hour or so, costs the same as a subway fare, takes about ten minutes and gives you the harbor, the skyline and the North End waterfront from the water. Take it. The alternative is Community College station on the Orange Line, a ten-minute walk downhill from the monument, or the walk back over the bridge to North Station, about fifteen minutes.

Bring a photo ID if you are 18 or over and want to board the ship, comfortable shoes because the North End and Charlestown are cobbled in places, and water in summer. The trail is walkable in any season. In winter, the burying grounds close earlier and the ship's hours shrink; in summer the North End feasts in July and August fill Hanover Street with processions and stalls, which slows you down in the best way. FAQ

Freedom Trail challenge: common questions

What is the Freedom Trail challenge?

The Freedom Trail challenge is the 2.5-mile Freedom Trail done as a self-guided checklist: all 16 official stops in order, from Boston Common to the Bunker Hill Monument, ticked off one by one as you reach them. You can follow it from this page or as a challenge in the no bad days club app, where each stop is a checklist item on a map.

How long does it take to walk the whole Freedom Trail?

About two hours if you walk it without going inside anywhere, three to four hours at a normal pace with a lunch stop and one or two paid sites, and a full day if you go inside everything and climb the Bunker Hill Monument. The walk itself is 2.5 miles, with the last mile from the North End to Charlestown the quietest.

Is the Freedom Trail free?

Walking the trail is free, and 11 of the 16 stops are free to enter or are outdoor sites. The Old South Meeting House, Old State House, Paul Revere House and Old North Church charge admission. King's Chapel asks for a suggested donation. USS Constitution and the Bunker Hill Monument are free; the monument climb may need a timed pass in busy seasons.

Where does the Freedom Trail start and end?

It starts at Boston Common, at the Visitor Information Center at 139 Tremont Street next to Park Street station, and ends at the Bunker Hill Monument in Charlestown. You can walk it in reverse, but the traditional direction saves the harbor ferry back from the Charlestown Navy Yard for the end.

How do you get back from the end of the Freedom Trail?

The easiest way is the MBTA ferry from the Charlestown Navy Yard pier, near the USS Constitution, to Long Wharf, which costs the same as a subway ride and takes about ten minutes. Otherwise, Community College station on the Orange Line is a ten-minute walk from the Bunker Hill Monument.

Can you do the Freedom Trail challenge with kids?

Yes. The Downtown stretch from the Common to Faneuil Hall is flat, about a mile, and full of things to spot. Most families do the Common to the North End section, roughly two miles, with a cannoli stop, and skip the Charlestown mile or take the ferry across to see the ship. The full trail is a long day for children under eight.

Is there an official Freedom Trail app or do you need a guide?

You do not need either. The red brick line in the sidewalk marks the whole route, and this page lists every stop with its address. The Freedom Trail Foundation runs paid costumed tours and the National Park Service runs free ranger walks of part of the trail in season, both starting near Faneuil Hall. The no bad days club app has the trail as a self-guided challenge with a map and checklist.
