Boston is the rare American city you can explore properly on foot. Downtown, the North End, Beacon Hill, Back Bay and the Seaport all sit within a 40-minute walk of each other, and the Charles River, the harbor and two big public parks fill in the gaps. Four hundred years of history are painted onto the sidewalk as a red brick line, the museums are among the best in the country, and the food scene has moved well past chowder without abandoning it.

This is the hub for everything we have written about Boston. There are four main guides: things to do, food and drink, neighborhoods and a set of self-guided city routes. Below those are itineraries for a weekend or three days, a solo travel guide, a bucket list, and a guide for people who have just moved here.

If you are visiting, start with the itineraries. If you live here, start with things to do and pick a mood. Either way, everything in these guides is a real place with an address and the nearest T stop, chosen because we would send a friend there.

03

How to explore Boston

By neighborhood. This is how locals think about the city, and it is the best way to explore Boston on foot. Each neighborhood is 15 to 30 minutes end to end, and most border another one worth walking into. A classic first day is Beacon Hill to Downtown to the North End, all on foot, with dinner on Hanover Street. A classic second day is Back Bay, the Fenway museums and the Esplanade. Our neighborhoods hub has a comparison table and a page for each, starting with the North End, Beacon Hill, Back Bay, the Seaport and Harvard Square.

By interest. History people should walk the Freedom Trail and finish at the USS Constitution. Art people have the MFA, the Gardner, the ICA and the free Harvard Art Museums. Sports people have Fenway Park tours year-round and games April to October. Food people should read the food and drink hub and plan around a lobster roll, an oyster bar and one North End dinner. People who just want to be outside have the Esplanade, the Harborwalk, the Arboretum and the harbor islands.

By time available. With a few hours, walk from Boston Common through Downtown to the North End. With a day, add Beacon Hill in the morning and the Seaport or Back Bay in the afternoon. With a weekend, use the weekend itinerary. With three days, use the 3-day itinerary, which adds Cambridge and a harbor trip. Anything longer, and you can start on the day trips: Salem, Concord, Provincetown by ferry, or the North Shore beaches.

05

Getting around Boston

Walk first. Boston's central neighborhoods are closer together than any map suggests: Downtown to the North End is 10 minutes, to the Seaport 15, to Copley Square 20, and to Harvard Square about an hour along the river. Most of the things-to-do guides are organized so you can walk between entries.

For anything further, take the T. The subway is $2.40 per ride with a CharlieCard or a tapped contactless card, a 7-day pass is $22.50, and the four lines meet Downtown at Park Street, Downtown Crossing, State and Government Center. The Red Line goes to Cambridge and Harvard, the Green Line to Back Bay, Fenway and the museums, the Blue Line to the airport and East Boston, and the Orange Line to the South End, Jamaica Plain and Charlestown. The Silver Line SL1 is free from the airport into the Seaport and South Station. Trains stop around 12:30am.

The ferry from Long Wharf to Charlestown costs the same as a subway ride and is the cheapest harbor view in the city. Bluebikes are everywhere and the Esplanade and Southwest Corridor are good car-free routes. Do not rent a car unless you are leaving the city: parking Downtown runs $30 to $50 a day, the streets were laid out for cows, and the drivers are exactly as advertised.

FAQ

Exploring Boston: common questions

How many days do you need to explore Boston?

Two full days covers the essentials: the Freedom Trail, the North End, Beacon Hill, Back Bay and one museum. Three days lets you add Cambridge and Harvard, the Seaport and a harbor trip. Boston is compact, so even one day is worthwhile if you stay central. See the weekend and 3-day itineraries.

What is the best way to get around Boston?

Walking, then the T. The central neighborhoods are all within a 30 to 40 minute walk of each other, and the subway costs $2.40 per ride. You do not need a car, and parking downtown is expensive and scarce.

When is the best time to visit Boston?

Late September through October for foliage, mild weather and the Head of the Charles Regatta, or May and June before the summer humidity. Winter is cold and can be icy, but hotels are cheaper and the museums are quiet.

Which Boston neighborhood should a first-time visitor stay in?

Back Bay or Downtown for first-timers: both are walkable to most sights and have the most T lines. Beacon Hill is quieter and prettier, the Seaport is newer with harbor views, and Cambridge suits anyone visiting Harvard or MIT. Our neighborhoods guide compares them.

Is Boston expensive to visit?

Hotels are the expensive part, especially in September, October and graduation season in May. Getting around and sightseeing is cheap: the Freedom Trail, the parks, the Harborwalk and several museum nights are free, and the T is $2.40 a ride.

Is Boston safe for walking at night?

The central neighborhoods covered in these guides are busy and well-lit into the evening. Use normal city sense, and note that the T stops running around 12:30am, so plan your route home if you are out late. See things to do in Boston at night.
