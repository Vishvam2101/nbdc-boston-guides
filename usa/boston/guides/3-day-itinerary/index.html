Three days is the right amount of time for Boston. The city is small: the Freedom Trail, the North End, the waterfront, Back Bay, Beacon Hill and the Esplanade all sit inside a two-mile circle, and Cambridge is one Red Line stop across the river. In three days you can walk almost all of it, eat well every night and still have a half-day left for the harbor or Salem, without ever renting a car.

This Boston itinerary for 3 days is built the way we would plan it for a friend. Day one is the historic core, walked north to south along the Freedom Trail and ending with dinner in the North End. Day two is the elegant side of the city: Back Bay, Beacon Hill, the Public Garden, a museum, and sunset on the Charles. Day three is a choice between Cambridge and the Seaport, or a half-day out on the Harbor Islands or in Salem. Each day has an hour-by-hour table, then the key stops with addresses and the nearest T stop.

Times assume a spring, summer or fall visit and a 9am start. If you arrive on the evening before day one, you are ahead of schedule; if you only have a weekend, the Boston weekend itinerary compresses this into two and a half days.

Day 1: Freedom Trail, North End and the Harborwalk

The classic first day, and the one that makes the rest of the city make sense. About five miles of flat walking, no T needed until bedtime. Start early: the Freedom Trail sites are quiet before 10am and packed after noon.

The Freedom Trail

Starts at Boston Common Visitor Center, 139 Tremont StPark Street (Red/Green)2.5 miles, 16 sites, about 3 hours walkingFree to walk

The trail is a line of red bricks in the sidewalk that runs from the Common to Bunker Hill, and you can follow it without a map. Walk it in order: the State House and the Granary Burying Ground (Paul Revere, Samuel Adams and John Hancock are all here) come first, then King's Chapel, the Old South Meeting House where the Tea Party was planned, and the Old State House, where the Declaration of Independence was first read to Bostonians from the balcony. The Boston Massacre site is the cobblestone ring in the traffic island outside. Our Freedom Trail guide covers every stop in detail.

Tip: the National Park Service runs free ranger-led walks from Faneuil Hall in season. They are good, but they only cover the downtown half. Doing it yourself lets you keep going into the North End without a group.

The North End: Paul Revere House, Old North Church, Copp's Hill

Paul Revere House, 19 North Sq · Old North Church, 193 Salem StHaymarket (Green/Orange)Both open daily in season, check before you goAbout $6 and $10

The North End is Boston's oldest residential neighborhood and its Italian one, and the two identities sit on top of each other: Paul Revere's 1680 house is a block from the espresso bars. The Old North Church is where the two lanterns were hung in April 1775, and the box pews inside are original. Copp's Hill Burying Ground behind it has the best view down to the Navy Yard. Read our North End neighborhood guide for the streets worth wandering between stops.

The Harborwalk from Long Wharf to Rowes Wharf

Long Wharf, 1 Long WharfAquarium (Blue)Always openFree

Take the ferry back rather than walking the bridge: it is a ten-minute ride that drops you at Long Wharf with the skyline in front of you. From there the Harborwalk runs south past the New England Aquarium and the seals in the outdoor tank, through the arch at Rowes Wharf, and on toward the Seaport if you want it. Turn around at Rowes Wharf and walk back to Christopher Columbus Park for the last light. It is among the best views in Boston and it costs nothing.

Day 2: Back Bay, Beacon Hill, the Public Garden and a museum

Day two is the Boston of brownstones, gas lamps and the Charles. It is a slower day with less mileage, and it ends with the best sunset in the city. If the Red Sox are at home, book tickets for the evening now; the Esplanade is a 15-minute walk from Fenway.

Boston Public Library, Copley Square and Newbury Street

700 Boylston StCopley (Green)Mon–Thu 9–8, Fri–Sat 9–5, Sun 11–5Free

The 1895 McKim building is the most beautiful public room in the city. Go straight up the marble staircase to Bates Hall, then to the Sargent Gallery on the third floor, then down to the courtyard, which has a café in warm months. Across the square, Trinity Church is H.H. Richardson's masterpiece, and the John Hancock Tower reflects it. Newbury Street runs eight blocks from the Public Garden to Mass Ave; the shops get more interesting the farther west you go. Our Back Bay guide has the full neighborhood.

The Public Garden and Beacon Hill

Public Garden, 4 Charles St · Acorn St, Beacon HillArlington (Green) or Charles/MGH (Red)Swan Boats run mid-April to early SeptemberGarden free; Swan Boats about $4.50

The Public Garden is the oldest public botanical garden in the US, and the Swan Boats have been pedaled around its lagoon since 1877. The Make Way for Ducklings bronzes are in the northeast corner near Beacon and Charles. Cross Charles Street and you are on Beacon Hill: gas lamps, brick sidewalks, and Acorn Street, the most photographed lane in the city. Louisburg Square is the grandest address; the Black Heritage Trail through the north slope tells the story of Boston's 19th-century free Black community. See Beacon Hill for lunch options on Charles Street.

Museum of Fine Arts, or the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum

MFA, 465 Huntington Ave · Gardner, 25 Evans WayMuseum of Fine Arts (Green E)Both closed Tuesdays; check hours before you goMFA about $27, Gardner about $22

They are three minutes apart, so choose by temperament. The MFA is one of the largest art museums in the country and needs three hours for even a fraction: the Art of the Americas wing, the Japanese collection and the Impressionists are the core. The Gardner is a Venetian palace around a courtyard garden, hung exactly as its founder left it, with the empty frames from the 1990 theft still on the walls; it takes ninety minutes and stays with you longer. Both are in the best museums in Boston.

Sunset on the Charles River Esplanade

Hatch Memorial Shell, 47 David G. Mugar WayCharles/MGH (Red) or Arlington (Green)Always openFree

The Esplanade is a three-mile park along the Boston bank of the Charles. The dock beside the Hatch Shell faces west, straight down the river toward the sunset, with the Cambridge skyline and the Longfellow Bridge to your right. In summer there are sailboats on the water and half the city is running past. Get there thirty minutes before sunset.

Harvard Yard and the Harvard Art Museums

Harvard Yard, Massachusetts Ave · Harvard Art Museums, 32 Quincy StHarvard (Red)Museums Tue–Sun 10–5, check before you goYard free; museums free

Harvard Yard is open to anyone: walk in through the Johnston Gate, past the John Harvard statue, and sit on the steps of Widener Library. The Harvard Art Museums next door have been free since 2023 and hold one of the best university collections in the world under a Renzo Piano glass roof. The Museum of Natural History's glass flowers are the alternative for families. Our Harvard Square guide has the bookstores and lunch options.

MIT and the Charles River

MIT, 77 Massachusetts Ave, Cambridge · Paddle Boston, 15 Broad Canal WayKendall/MIT (Red)Campus always open; kayaks May–OctoberCampus free; kayak from about $20 per hour, check before you go

MIT's campus is a mile of odd architecture: Frank Gehry's Stata Center, the Simmons Hall "sponge," and the Great Dome over Killian Court, which has the best straight-on view of the Boston skyline. In warm months the kayak rental on Broad Canal puts you on the Charles in five minutes, and the walk back over the Longfellow Bridge is the city's best free view of both skylines at once.

The Seaport and the ICA

Institute of Contemporary Art, 25 Harbor Shore DrCourthouse (Silver Line SL1/SL2) or South Station (Red)Wed–Sun, Thu until 9pm; check before you goAbout $20; free Thursdays 5–9pm

The Seaport was parking lots twenty years ago and is now the newest neighborhood in the city, with the ICA cantilevered over the harbor at the end of it. The museum takes ninety minutes and the fourth-floor glass gallery facing the water is the point. Fort Point, across the channel, is the older brick side with the better restaurants: Row 34 for oysters, Drink for cocktails. More in our Seaport guide.

Salem, by commuter rail or ferry

Peabody Essex Museum, 161 Essex St, Salem · House of the Seven Gables, 115 Derby StNorth Station (Green/Orange) to Salem, Newburyport/Rockport Line, 30 minFerry from Long Wharf runs late May to October, check before you goTrain about $8 each way; PEM about $20

Salem is two towns at once: the 1692 witch-trial town of the memorials and wax museums, and the wealthy 1800s port of Chestnut Street and the Peabody Essex Museum, which is far better than a town of 45,000 has any right to have. See the PEM, walk Derby Street to the House of the Seven Gables, and skip the tourist traps. Avoid October unless crowds are the point. Our Salem day-trip guide has the full plan.

Where to stay for this itinerary

The right base for a 3-day Boston itinerary puts day one on foot: Downtown, the waterfront or the edge of the North End. Back Bay is the runner-up and nicer at night. Prices are typical ranges; check before you book.

Downtown and the waterfront: best for day one

Omni Parker House, 60 School St · The Bostonian, 26 North St · Boston Harbor Hotel, 70 Rowes WharfPark Street, Haymarket, AquariumRoughly $250–700 a night

The Parker House is on the Freedom Trail itself and the Bostonian faces Faneuil Hall with the North End behind it; both let you start day one from the lobby. The Boston Harbor Hotel is the splurge, with rooms on the water at Rowes Wharf. For less, HI Boston (19 Stuart St) is a modern hostel a block from the Common, and citizenM at North Station (70 Causeway St) is small-room, well designed and a 15-minute walk from the North End.

Back Bay: best for the whole trip

The Lenox, 61 Exeter St · Copley Square Hotel, 47 Huntington Ave · The Newbury, 1 Newbury StCopley or Arlington (Green)Roughly $250–800 a night

Back Bay puts the library, Newbury Street, the Public Garden and the Esplanade within a ten-minute walk, and the Green Line reaches Park Street for day one in four stops. The Lenox is the reliable classic; the Newbury is the grand hotel on the corner of the Garden. Full options in where to stay in Boston.

06

Variations: with kids, in the rain, in winter, for sports fans

With kids

Keep the shape, shorten the walks. Do the downtown half of the Freedom Trail, then the ferry to Charlestown for the USS Constitution, which children love. On day two the Swan Boats and the ducklings are the anchor; swap the MFA for the Museum of Science (1 Science Park, Science Park/West End on the Green Line). On day three, replace Cambridge with the Boston Children's Museum (308 Congress St) and the New England Aquarium (1 Central Wharf), a 15-minute Harborwalk apart. A Duck Boat tour gives a child the whole city in eighty minutes. See Boston with kids.

On a rainy day

Move the MFA or the Gardner to the wet day and add the library, which is free and indoors. Faneuil Hall and Quincy Market are covered, the Aquarium is a good two hours, and the Harvard museums and the ICA both work. The Freedom Trail is fine in light rain; the Harborwalk and the Esplanade are not, so swap them for an early dinner and a film at the Coolidge or the Brattle. Our rainy-day guide ranks the options.

In winter

Start the Freedom Trail at 10am for the sun on the brick and take the T to Charlestown instead of the bridge. Swan Boats, harbor ferries and kayaks do not run, so day three becomes Cambridge plus the Seaport, or a full museum day. Add skating on the Frog Pond on the Common (rentals about $15, check before you go) and, in December, the lights on Commonwealth Avenue. More in Boston in winter.

For sports fans

Check the schedules first. A Red Sox game slots into day two as written. The Bruins and Celtics play at TD Garden (100 Legends Way, North Station) from October to April, ten minutes' walk from the North End, so a game fits after day one if you eat early. The Fenway Park tour is worth it out of season, and if you are here on Patriots' Day in April, the Marathon finish on Boylston Street is the whole day.

07

Getting around Boston for 3 days

You will walk most of this: about five miles on day one, four on day two. The T covers the rest: the Green Line for Back Bay, the museums and Fenway; the Red Line for Cambridge and South Station; the Blue Line for the Aquarium and the airport; the Orange and Green lines for North Station and Haymarket. A subway ride is $2.40 with a CharlieCard or a tapped contactless card, a one-day pass is $11 and a seven-day pass is $22.50, which pays for itself on this trip. Trains run from about 5am to around 12:30am.

From Logan, the Silver Line SL1 bus to South Station is free from the airport and takes about twenty minutes; the Blue Line from Airport station serves Downtown and the waterfront. Taxis to the center run $25 to $40. Commuter rail to Salem leaves from North Station, and the Charlestown ferry is part of the T. Details in getting around Boston.

08

Before you go: booking, timing and small things

Book ahead for three things: Red Sox tickets, weekend Fenway tours, and dinner at any North End restaurant that takes reservations. Everything else can be decided the morning of. The MFA and the Gardner are closed on Tuesdays and the USS Constitution is closed Monday and Tuesday most of the year, so check which day of your trip falls where and swap days if you need to.

The best months are late April through June and September through October; July and August are humid and crowded. Marathon Monday, the third Monday in April, fills every hotel. Many North End restaurants are cash-only, tipping is 18 to 20 percent, and the weather changes by the hour, so pack a layer. FAQ

Boston itinerary for 3 days: common questions

Is 3 days enough for Boston?

Yes. Three days covers the Freedom Trail, the North End, Back Bay and Beacon Hill, one major museum, the waterfront and a half-day in Cambridge, with time for a proper dinner every night. A fourth day is only needed if you want a full day trip to Cape Cod or the Harbor Islands.

What is the best order to see Boston in 3 days?

Do the Freedom Trail and North End on day one, because it is the walk everything else is measured against. Day two is Back Bay, Beacon Hill and a museum, which are close together. Save Cambridge, the Seaport or a day trip for day three, when you already know the city.

Do I need a car for 3 days in Boston?

No. Every stop in this itinerary is walkable or on the T, and parking in central Boston runs $40 to $60 a day. Even the Salem option works by commuter rail or ferry. Only rent a car if you are extending the trip to Cape Cod or the Berkshires.

How much does 3 days in Boston cost?

Excluding flights, plan on roughly $150 a day for a budget trip staying in a hostel, $350 to $450 a day at a mid-range hotel with sit-down dinners, and $700 or more a day for a harbor-view hotel and tasting-menu dining. Transit is under $25 for the whole three days on a 7-day pass.

What is the best area to stay for a 3-day Boston itinerary?

Downtown or the edge of the North End puts you at the start of the Freedom Trail and within a 15-minute walk of the waterfront. Back Bay is the best alternative: quieter, closer to the museums and the Esplanade, and every day of this plan is one short Green Line ride away.

Can I do this Boston itinerary in winter?

Yes, with adjustments. Walk the Freedom Trail in the late-morning sun, swap the Harbor Islands or Salem for a full day of museums, add skating on the Frog Pond, and replace Fenway with a Bruins or Celtics game at TD Garden. Swan Boats and harbor ferries do not run in winter.
