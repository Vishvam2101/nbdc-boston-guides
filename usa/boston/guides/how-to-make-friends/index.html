How to make friends in Boston.
Slower than you'd like, but it sticks.

An honest guide for people who moved to Boston in their 20s: why the first months are hard, the channels that actually work to meet people, and a plan for your first 90 days.

By no bad days club, BostonUpdated September 8, 202614 min read

Nobody warns you about the second month. The first month after you move to Boston is busy: the lease, the CharlieCard, the IKEA trip, figuring out which Market Basket is yours. Then the boxes are unpacked, the people you knew from college have texted back exactly once, and you realize you have spent four consecutive evenings walking around the Esplanade by yourself. That is normal. It is also fixable, and this guide is about how.

Boston has a reputation for being cold, and not only in February. People here are polite, reserved and busy, and a large share of them leave after a degree, a residency or a two-year rotation. But the city is also small, packed with people in their 20s and 30s, and unusually rich in weekly recurring activities where turning up alone is expected. The trick is not finding people. It is finding the same people, repeatedly, until they stop being strangers.

What follows is every channel we have seen work for people who moved here without a built-in crowd, with the specific groups, addresses and T stops, then a neighborhood-by-neighborhood note and a first-90-days plan. If you are still unpacking, start with just moved to Boston and come back.

01

Why making friends in Boston is hard at first

It helps to know what you are up against, because none of it is about you. Three things make Boston harder than, say, Austin or Denver for a newcomer.

It is transient. Greater Boston has more than 35 colleges and universities and a huge hospital and biotech workforce. A large share of the people you meet in your first year are here for a two-year master's, a residency, a postdoc or a consulting rotation, and they know it. That makes people slower to invest in new friendships, and it means some of the friends you do make will leave. Plan for it: aim for a wider circle than you think you need.

It is reserved. Boston is a Northeastern city with old social habits. Strangers do not chat on the T, nobody says hello on the sidewalk, and a lot of long-time residents still have the friend group they made in high school or at BC. This is not hostility. It is a city where friendliness is shown by showing up, not by small talk. The good news is that once you are inside an activity, the reserve drops fast.

Winter is long. From roughly mid-December to late March, people go from work to home and stay there. If you arrive in September, as most people do, you have about ten weeks of good weather to build a base before the city goes indoors. Use them, and plan indoor anchors (a league, a gym, a class) for the dark months so your social life does not reset in January.

Add the practical stuff, and it compounds: half the people in their 20s here live in Allston, Somerville and Cambridge, and if you took an apartment in the Seaport or the Financial District, you may be a 40-minute Green Line ride from where the casual stuff happens. Neighborhood matters more here than in a car city. More on that below.

02

Run clubs and rec sports leagues

Boston is a running city with a marathon at the center of its calendar, and its run clubs are free, weekly and built for strangers. Rec sports leagues are the other engine: you pay a fee, get put on a team of people you have never met, and see them every week for two months.

November Project

Harvard Stadium, 65 N Harvard St, Allston (Wed) · Various locations (Mon and Fri)Harvard (Red) then a 15-min walk, or the 66 busWednesdays 6:30am, year-roundFree

November Project started in Boston in 2011 as two friends promising to work out together through the winter, and it is now a worldwide free fitness movement whose home base is still the Harvard Stadium stairs on Wednesday mornings. The format is designed for newcomers: you are told to hug the person next to you, everybody's name gets shouted, and there is a "newbie" welcome every single week. It is loud and earnest, and it is the single fastest way to know 20 people in Boston. Show up three Wednesdays in a row and someone will invite you to breakfast.

Tip: The Friday workouts move around the city and are smaller and more conversational than Wednesday's stadium session. Check their social channels for the week's location.

Heartbreak Hill Running Company runs

652 Tremont St, South End · 61 N Beacon St, Boston (Brighton)Back Bay (Orange) for the South End storeWeekly group runs, typically Tuesday and Thursday evenings; check the store's scheduleFree

Heartbreak Hill is a running store with a club attached, and its free evening group runs from the South End store are a good bridge between November Project's intensity and a normal social run. Pace groups are split, nobody is dropped, and the run usually ends at a bar. It skews 25 to 40 and is heavy on people who moved here recently.

Tracksmith Trackhouse runs

285 Newbury St, Back BayHynes Convention Center (Green)Weekly group runs from the store; check the schedule before you goFree

Tracksmith's Newbury Street store hosts regular group runs along the Charles that draw a slightly more serious runner than the neighborhood clubs. It is a good pick if you already run and want training partners rather than a party. The post-run crowd tends to linger.

Slow AF Run Club and no-drop groups

Meeting points vary, often the Esplanade or Cambridge side of the riverCharles/MGH (Red)Check the group's page for this week's meetFree

If the words "run club" make you picture people doing six-minute miles, look for the explicitly slow, no-drop groups. Slow AF Run Club has a Boston chapter, and several neighborhood groups (JP, Somerville, Southie) run at conversational pace and are upfront about welcoming walkers. The whole point is talking, which is exactly what you want.

Social Boston Sports and Volo leagues

Fields and gyms across Boston, Cambridge and Somerville; sponsor bars nearbyVaries by leagueSeasons of 6 to 8 weeks, year-round including indoor winter leaguesRoughly $60 to $100 per season as a free agent; check current pricing

This is the channel we recommend most to people who are not runners. Social Boston Sports and Volo both run co-ed leagues in kickball, softball, volleyball, dodgeball, bocce, cornhole, flag football and soccer. Sign up as a "free agent" and you are placed on a team with other free agents, which means everyone on your team is also new and also looking. Games are one night a week and end at a sponsor bar. Kickball and bocce are the least athletic and the most social; volleyball and soccer draw people who actually want to play.

Tip: Register early for the fall season (sign-ups open in August) and the winter indoor season. Free agent spots go first.

03

Trivia, board game nights and climbing gyms

The weeknight channels. Each of these works because the activity gives you something to do with your hands and a reason to talk that is not "so, where are you from?"

Weekly pub trivia, same bar every week

The Burren, 247 Elm St, Somerville · Silhouette Lounge, 200 Brighton Ave, Allston · Trident Booksellers, 338 Newbury St · many moreDavis (Red) · Packard's Corner (Green B) · Hynes (Green)Most weeknights, usually 7 or 8pmFree to play

Trivia is the most underrated friendship engine in Boston, because the rules are the same everywhere: tell the host you are alone, and they will put you on a team. The mistake people make is going once. Pick one bar, go every week, and by week four the team you were placed on is your team. Trident's Tuesday trivia in Back Bay is especially newcomer-heavy. Our trivia guide lists nights by neighborhood.

Board game night at Knight Moves

1402 Beacon St, BrooklineCoolidge Corner (Green C)Open most days into the evening; cover charge for open play, check current hours and price

Knight Moves is a board game café with a library of over a thousand games and a paid open-play model, which means everyone in the room has decided to spend the evening playing games with people. Staff will match solo visitors to tables that need a player, and the regular game nights (the Cambridge location on Mass Ave runs them too) are explicitly for strangers. Two hours of a cooperative game with three people is worth six happy hours.

A climbing gym membership

Central Rock Gym, 74 Cambridge St, Cambridge · Brooklyn Boulders (now BKB), 12A Tyler St, SomervilleLechmere (Green) · Union Square (Green D)Day passes around $25 to $30, memberships from about $90 a month; check current rates

Bouldering is the most social workout that exists. You climb for 30 seconds, then stand around for five minutes talking about the route with whoever is trying the same one. Go at the same time twice a week, and within a month you have a crew. Central Rock's Cambridge location near Lechmere is the biggest and busiest; BKB in Somerville has a coworking area and a slightly artier crowd. Both run intro classes that put you in a group of beginners on day one.

04

Volunteering: the most reliable channel nobody uses

A weekly volunteer shift is the only channel on this page where you are guaranteed to see the same 10 people every week, working on something together, with nothing to buy. It is slower to turn into friendship than a run club and much more durable.

Community Servings kitchen shifts

179 Amory St, Jamaica PlainStony Brook (Orange)Multiple shifts daily; sign up in advance onlineFree

Community Servings cooks medically tailored meals for people with serious illness, and its volunteer kitchen runs shifts every day. You chop vegetables alongside 15 to 20 other people for three hours, and the shift leads are good at getting people talking. Many companies send teams, but the weekday evening and Saturday shifts draw individuals. Book a recurring slot.

Rosie's Place

889 Harrison Ave, South EndMassachusetts Avenue (Orange)Kitchen and dining room shifts; orientation required firstFree

Rosie's Place is a sanctuary for poor and homeless women, and its dining room and food pantry rely on volunteers for lunch and dinner service. The orientation is short, the work is meaningful, and the regular volunteer pool is a mix of ages and neighborhoods you will not find at a kickball league.

Cradles to Crayons

155 N Beacon St, BrightonBoston Landing (Commuter Rail) or the 64 busTwo-hour sorting shifts most days; register onlineFree

Cradles to Crayons sorts donated clothing, books and school supplies for kids in need at its Brighton warehouse, and its two-hour shifts are the easiest volunteer commitment in the city: no orientation beyond a five-minute briefing, drop-in registration, and a room full of other people in their 20s who also just moved here. Good for a first attempt at volunteering.

05

Classes and language exchanges

Cambridge Center for Adult Education

42 Brattle St, CambridgeHarvard (Red)Evening and weekend courses, 1 to 10 weeksMost courses $50 to $300; check the catalog

The CCAE has been running adult classes out of a house on Brattle Street since 1938: cooking, pottery, languages, writing, woodworking, drawing, wine. The rule for friend-making is to pick a multi-week course, not a one-night workshop, and to pick something hands-on where the class talks to each other. A six-week Italian conversation or pottery course is a built-in weekly meeting with the same 10 people. The Boston Center for Adult Education in the South End runs a similar program on the other side of the river.

Language exchanges

Cafés and bars around Harvard Square, Kendall and Back Bay; locations announced weeklyHarvard or Kendall (Red)Weekly, mostly eveningsFree or the cost of a drink

With one of the largest international student and researcher populations in the country, Boston has weekly language exchange nights in Spanish, French, Portuguese, Mandarin, Japanese and more, most of them organized through Meetup or university groups. You do not need to be fluent. The format is explicitly for meeting strangers, half the room is also new to the city, and it is one of the best ways to build a circle that does not look exactly like your office.

Cooking classes and studio memberships

Boston Center for Adult Education, 122 Arlington St · Mudflat Studio, 81 Broadway, SomervilleArlington (Green) · Sullivan Square (Orange)

Pottery studios in particular have become a quiet social scene in Boston, because a membership means you show up at odd hours and see the same people at the wheel. Mudflat in Somerville has a long waitlist for its classes; get on it in your first month. Cooking classes are more one-off but easy to attend alone.

06

Meetup, Bumble BFF and coworking

Meetup groups

Citywide; the active groups are hiking, board games, "new in Boston," 20s and 30s socials, and photography walks

Meetup in Boston is unusually alive because of the churn of newcomers. The groups that work are the ones with a fixed weekly or monthly activity: a Saturday hike in the Blue Hills, a Thursday board game night, a monthly photo walk. The "20s and 30s Boston social" groups are hit or miss, since a bar meetup with 60 strangers is harder than a hike with 12. Filter for groups that have met at least ten times.

Bumble BFF

Free with optional paid tiers

Bumble BFF has a real user base in Boston, mostly women in their 20s and 30s who moved here for work or grad school. The people who find it useful treat it as a way to find an activity partner, not a friend: "want to try the Thursday trivia at Trident?" beats "hey, how's your week going?" every time. Suggest a specific thing in the first three messages, and go to a recurring activity so that the second meeting is already scheduled.

Coworking and work-from-home cafés

Workbar, multiple locations · CIC, 1 Broadway, Cambridge · Render Coffee, 563 Columbus AveKendall (Red) for CIC

If you work remotely, your office is not going to introduce you to anyone, so you need to manufacture one. Coworking spaces like Workbar and CIC run member socials, and the coffee-machine conversation is real. Cheaper: pick one café and work from it on the same two mornings every week. Regulars notice regulars. Our list of the best cafés to work from covers where that is welcome.

07

Hobby communities: sailing, rowing, skiing

Boston has a few hobby institutions that are unusually good at absorbing newcomers, because they are cheap, structured and depend on members teaching each other.

Community Boating on the Charles

21 David G. Mugar Way, the EsplanadeCharles/MGH (Red)April to October; check season datesAdult season memberships from roughly $200 to $400; check current rates

Community Boating is the oldest public sailing program in the country, and an adult membership gets you unlimited sailing on the Charles for a season plus free lessons taught by other members. You learn in small groups, you crew for people with more experience, and the dock on a summer evening is a standing social scene. It is the single best summer channel on this list, and it is also how you get a view of the skyline that most residents never see. See it on our best views list.

Learn-to-row programs

Community Rowing, 20 Nonantum Rd, Brighton · Riverside Boat Club, CambridgeBoston Landing (Commuter Rail) or the 64 busSpring through fall; learn-to-row sessions run several times a season

Community Rowing's adult learn-to-row program puts you in a boat with seven strangers at 6am and requires you to move in unison. Nothing bonds people faster. Sessions are a few weeks long and feed into recreational crews that row together all season.

Boston Ski & Sports Club

Bus trips depart from central Boston and Cambridge; social events citywideWinter ski trips, summer sports leagues and social calendar year-round

BSSC has run ski bus trips to New Hampshire and Vermont for decades, and a day on the bus is a day of forced proximity with 50 people who all like the same thing. It also runs its own sports leagues and travel trips, and it is the winter answer to "what do I do from January to March." A membership pays for itself if you go on two trips.

Hiking and the outdoors

Appalachian Mountain Club, 10 City Square, Charlestown · Blue Hills Reservation, MiltonAshmont (Red) then the 240 bus for Blue Hills

The Appalachian Mountain Club's Boston chapter runs beginner-friendly hikes almost every weekend, many of them reachable without a car. The Blue Hills are a 40-minute trip from Downtown, and a Saturday hike with a small group is four hours of conversation. Winter hikes exist too, and the people on them are committed.

08

Church, temple and mosque communities

If faith is part of your life, this is the channel that has worked longest and most reliably, and Boston has an unusually strong set of young-adult congregations because of the student population. Most large churches, synagogues and mosques run explicit young-adult groups with weekly dinners, small groups and service projects, which is the recurring-activity model this whole guide is built on.

A few known for their 20s and 30s communities: Trinity Church in Copley Square (Episcopal, 206 Clarendon St, Copley on the Green Line), the Paulist Center on Park Street (Catholic, near Park Street station), Park Street Church (evangelical, at Park Street station), Temple Israel in the Longwood area (Reform, Longwood on the Green E), the Vilna Shul on Beacon Hill for cultural and young-adult Jewish programming, and the Islamic Society of Boston Cultural Center in Roxbury (100 Malcolm X Blvd, Roxbury Crossing on the Orange Line), the largest mosque in New England. Nearly all of them welcome newcomers by name at the door, which in Boston is rare.

If you are not religious but miss the structure, the Unitarian Universalist congregations (Arlington Street Church, First Church in Boston) are explicitly welcoming to the unsure, and the Boston Ethical Society runs Sunday gatherings with a similar community function.

Your first 90 days: a plan

Friendship research puts the cost of a casual friend at about 50 hours together and a close friend at around 200. That is why one-off events do not work and weekly recurring ones do. This plan is built to hit those hours without feeling like a project.

11

What actually makes it work

Recurrence beats novelty. Every channel in this guide that works has the same shape: the same people, the same place, the same time, every week. Every channel that does not work (big bar meetups, one-off events, "networking nights") lacks it. When you are choosing between two options, choose the one you will still be at in week six.

Say yes to the after-thing. The run is not where the friends are made. The diner after the run is. Trivia is not where the friends are made; the second round after the scores are read is. If you leave when the activity ends, you did the work and skipped the payoff.

Make the first move outside the activity. At some point, you have to text someone from the kickball team and suggest a thing that is not kickball. It feels forward. It is how everyone here who has friends got them. Boston's reserve means the other person is probably waiting for you to do it.

Treat winter as the season for depth, not breadth. Do not expect to meet many new people between December and March. Do expect the people you already know to become close, because there is nothing else to do. Board game nights, dinners, a ski trip with BSSC. The February friend is the one who is still around in five years.

Give it a year. The honest answer to "how long does it take" is longer than you want. Most people we know describe months one to three as rough, months four to six as fine, and the second September, when a new crop of people arrives and you are suddenly the one who knows where the good trivia is, as the moment it clicked. If you are eating alone in the meantime, Boston is one of the best cities for it; see things to do alone in Boston and the best bars to meet people. And if you are new to the whole city and not only its people, start with the Boston hub.

FAQ

How to make friends in Boston: common questions

Is it hard to make friends in Boston?

At first, yes. Boston is reserved, a lot of people leave after two or three years, and the winter keeps everyone indoors from December to March. But the city is small, dense with people in their 20s and 30s, and full of weekly recurring activities. If you show up to the same thing every week for two or three months, it gets much easier.

What is the best way to meet people in Boston in your 20s?

Recurring, low-commitment activities where strangers are expected: run clubs like November Project and Heartbreak Hill, rec sports leagues like Social Boston Sports and Volo, weekly pub trivia, climbing gyms, and volunteering shifts. The pattern matters more than the specific activity.

Which Boston neighborhoods are best for meeting people?

For people in their 20s: Allston, Brighton, Somerville (Davis and Union Square), Cambridge (Central and Inman), the South End and Southie. They have the highest density of roommates, run clubs, trivia nights and casual bars, and most of it is walkable.

How long does it take to make friends after moving to Boston?

Most people describe the first three to six months as lonely and the second six months as fine. Research on friendship suggests about 50 hours of shared time to become casual friends and 200 hours to become close, which is why weekly recurring activities work and one-off events do not.

Is Bumble BFF worth using in Boston?

It works better in Boston than in most cities because so many people are new and looking. Treat it as a way to find someone to go to a run club or trivia night with, not as the friendship itself, and suggest a specific activity in the first few messages.

How do I make friends in Boston in the winter?

Move your social life indoors and keep it weekly: climbing gyms, board game nights at Knight Moves, trivia, adult education classes, Boston Ski and Sports Club trips, indoor rec leagues, and volunteering shifts. Winter is when the people who stay in Boston bond with each other.
